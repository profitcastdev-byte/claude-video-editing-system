### 2.1 The Book Every Editor Has to Read: Walter Murch and *In the Blink of an Eye*

- **Source:** 2.1 · StudioBinder · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PKYeClvvlTw · 905 s · 2,093 words · direct match (match 1).
- **Thesis:** A cut works because it acts like a blink: it punctuates a completed thought. When choosing a cut, rank emotion first, story second and rhythm third, ahead of eye trace, the 180° line and spatial continuity. Getting the top items right hides problems lower down, but not the other way round.

**Rules**
1. Treat every cut as "a link in between two focuses of attention": know what the viewer is attending to before the cut and what they will attend to after it. [00:00]
2. A cut is a jump in space, time or both. Each cut must avoid confusing or distracting the viewer. [02:01]
3. Use juxtaposition on purpose. Viewers follow two placed images and also *make meaning* from the pairing. [02:32]
4. Cut when a thought is fully formed. The cut is the blink that "separates and punctuates" one idea from the next. [03:32]
5. Use the performer's blinks as cut-point candidates. Murch found his chosen cuts fell "within a few frames" of where Gene Hackman blinked, which signalled a completed thought. [04:00]
6. Feel for the cut point and test it repeatedly. The right frame is found again and again "much more than chance", so it is not arbitrary. [04:00]
7. Place cuts so that you "blink for the audience": the cut controls how the viewer processes information and underlines the ideas and emotions. [04:31]
8. Find the scene's rhythm. If it is right, the whole audience blinks (shifts attention) together. [04:31]
9. Anti-pattern, the "Dragnet system": a mechanical cut after every line gives a punchy rhythm but ignores the nuance of real conversation. [05:00]
10. Viewers accept an abrupt cut the way they accept juxtaposed images in a dream. You do not need to soften every cut. [05:31–06:01]
11. Use the Kuleshov effect: two images placed together create a third, separate meaning. [06:31]
12. The bee-hive principle: a cut to a shot *too similar* to the previous one is more jarring than a large change, because the viewer doesn't reorient. Avoid near-identical consecutive framings. [06:31–07:00]
13. The 30° rule of thumb: cut to a camera angle at least 30° away from the previous shot. [07:00]
14. Spatial considerations should *not* be the editor's primary concern. [07:30]
15. Rule of Six, priority 1, **Emotion**: preserve it "at all costs". For every cut ask how it makes you feel and whether that is the feeling the film needs at this point. [08:01]
16. Rule of Six, priority 2, **Story**: a cut must push the narrative forward. Ask whether the viewer understands the characters, their motivations and the plot. [08:32]
17. Rule of Six, priority 3, **Rhythm**: the cut must land at the right musical point. A cut can be "too late or too soon", rushing or dragging, like a drummer. [08:32–09:02]
18. Emotion, story and rhythm are tightly bound. Finding the emotional cut usually also advances the story and gives the right rhythm. [09:02]
19. Rule of Six, priority 4, **Eye trace**: put the incoming shot's focus where the viewer's eye already is. Upper right → upper right feels natural. [09:31]
20. Direct attention by choosing *where* you cut and where the focus of interest sits in the incoming shot. [09:31]
21. Rule of Six, priority 5, **Planarity (180° rule)**: shots cut together should generally not cross the line of action set by the opening shot of the sequence. [10:01]
22. Rule of Six, priority 6, **3D continuity**: keep where people are in the room, and in relation to each other, consistent. [10:01]
23. When priorities conflict, give up the lower ones. Satisfying higher items hides problems with lower items (eye trace, stage line, spatial continuity), "but not vice versa". [10:30]
24. The success test: the shots "seem to create each other". [11:00]
25. Have a plan before cutting. An editor makes thousands of decisions per scene. [11:00]
26. When dailies arrive, pick one still per shot that best represents it, and use these stills to choose takes later. [11:32]
27. Internalise rhythm physically (Murch edits standing up: "editing is a kind of dance"). [11:32–12:01]
28. Bring your own ideas on small details where the director's attention runs out. [12:01–12:31]
29. Judge only what is on screen, as the audience will. Ignore knowledge from the shoot (Murch avoids the set). [12:31]
30. Use test viewers to learn *that* something isn't working, not *why*. Audiences are poor at diagnosis. [13:01]
31. If ~80% of viewers agree one scene is their least favourite, don't reflexively fix or cut that scene. The cause is often missing information *earlier* that the scene needs. [13:31]
32. Almost all films are still built on simple cuts. Default to the cut. [14:01]
33. The editor's collaboration can start at script, storyboard or shot list. [14:01]

### 2.2 6 Ways to Edit Any Scene: Essential Film & Video Editing Techniques (Shot List Ep. 10)

- **Source:** 2.2 · StudioBinder · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FVR8zz8ci2k · 1,128 s · 1,990 words · direct match.
- **Thesis:** Editing comes down to three choices: what to show, when to cut and what to cut to. Six core within-scene techniques serve those choices: eyeline match, cross-cutting, eye trace, split edits (J/L), intellectual montage and cut on action.

**Rules**
1. Structure is nested: shots → scenes → sequences → film. [00:00]
2. Editing reduces to three choices: **what to show, when to cut, what to cut to**. [00:33]
3. The cut is the basic move between shots. Cutting away from the main action is a **cutaway**. [00:33]
4. Prioritise cut decisions with Murch's Rule of Six. [01:32]
5. Pace sets emotion, style and tone. For a mournful or contemplative scene, hold shots longer. [01:32]
6. For frantic action, cut rapidly. [02:01]
7. Use a cutaway to show what is on a character's mind. [02:01]
8. Or cut to what the character is looking at. [02:30]
9. Every edit must be "thoughtful and purposeful". [02:30]
10. **Eyeline match**: when a character notices something, cut to what they see. The second shot is often a POV or from their general perspective. [02:30–03:00]
11. Build intrigue by *delaying* the eyeline reveal, or by never showing it. Either way, the eyes motivate the cut. [03:00–03:33]
12. Let each glance drive a cut so the viewer thinks along with the character (300: glance → land, citizens, wife). [03:33–04:01]
13. **Shot / reverse shot**: cut between subject A and subject B at reverse angles of similar angle and composition, to keep scene geography and clarity in conversations. [04:31]
14. **Insert**: a detail shot that emphasises a specific prop or element, often prompted by an eyeline. [04:31–05:01]
15. Eyeline matches create and release tension depending on *what* is shown and *when*. [05:01]
16. **Cross-cutting**: cut between two or more scenes in different locations, usually happening at the same time. [06:02]
17. Use cross-cutting for ironic juxtaposition (Godfather baptism vs executions). [06:31]
18. Alternate between action fronts to keep the plot moving and the viewer invested in each thread. Cross-cutting creates parallels that would not exist otherwise. [07:30]
19. **Eye trace**: cut between shots whose focus sits in the same area of the frame, so the eye doesn't have to scan after the cut. [08:00]
20. Eye trace can also be guided inside a shot through blocking and camera movement. [08:31]
21. Eye trace matters most in fast cutting. In Mad Max: Fury Road the centre point of each shot was put in frame centre ("crosshairs on her nose"), because the viewer has ~1.8 s per shot and some shots are under 1 s. [09:00–09:32]
22. A **hard cut** changes audio and video together. A **split edit** changes one before the other. [10:01–10:36]
23. **J-cut**: the incoming shot's audio starts before its picture. [10:36]
24. **L-cut**: the outgoing shot's audio continues after the picture has cut away. [11:00]
25. Use J/L cuts in dialogue to make conversation feel natural, to control the rhythm of dialogue and to catch reaction shots of the listener. [11:31]
26. L-cut a described plan over visuals of the plan being executed. Seeing it is more interesting than hearing it, and it clarifies the plan. [12:00–13:02]
27. Add further J/L cuts within dialogue to keep a sequence moving swiftly. [13:02]
28. To make a cut *noticeable*, use **intellectual montage**: put seemingly unrelated images together to create a feeling or idea. [13:02]
29. Intellectual montage relies on the Kuleshov effect: the same expression reads differently depending on the intercut image. [13:37]
30. Juxtaposed images can tell the viewer something the dialogue doesn't (Lucy: "she is walking into a trap"). [14:00]
31. Shots that are neutral alone gain meaning in context. Use montage to restate a large theme (Don't Look Up). [15:01–15:31]
32. **Cut on action**: cut during a movement, with shot A's out-point matching shot B's in-point, so the transition is seamless. [15:31–16:01]
33. Cut on action works for simple actions (taking a drink, shaking hands) in any genre. [16:01]
34. Cut on action suits fight scenes, giving fast rhythm and quick movement. [16:32]
35. **Cut on impact**: place the cut mid-move or at the impact to give each blow extra force. [17:04]
36. Punch in from wide to a dramatic close-up on the action to keep the pace and strengthen the stunt. [17:31]
37. Other cut types exist: freeze frames, invisible cuts, subliminal frames, double cuts, jump cuts. [18:01]

**Count: 37 rules.**

### 2.3 Ultimate Guide to Scene Transitions: Every Editing Transition Explained (Shot List Ep. 9)

- **Source:** 2.3 · StudioBinder · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TKXBAaQB03U · 1,168 s · 2,310 words · direct match.
- **Thesis:** Each transition carries a meaning. A cut is neutral, a fade is closure or a pause, a dissolve is time or memory, a match cut is a connection, a wipe or iris is vintage or ironic, a pass-by or whip keeps momentum, a smash cut is a jolt and a J/L cut is a sound bridge. Pick the transition for its function, not for decoration.

**Rules**
1. Transitions connect scenes and move the viewer through the story. [00:01]
2. **Cut**: an instant switch between shots or scenes, and by far the most common transition. [01:30]
3. Cutting "jaggedly" can accentuate violence. Cut texture is expressive. [02:02]
4. **Fade**: a shot dissolves to or from a solid colour, usually black or white. [02:02]
5. Fade *from* black to open, easing the viewer into the story. [02:31]
6. Fade *to* black at the end for gradual closure, "like opening and closing a book". [02:31]
7. A mid-film fade to black ends a chapter. It deliberately pauses momentum so the viewer can breathe or reflect (Pulp Fiction between chapters). [03:02]
8. **Dip to black** inside a scene is a stylistic choice (Gone Girl). [03:36]
9. **Fade to white** is rarer: entering a dream, or dying. It can leave the ending ambiguous instead of closed (Black Swan). [04:00–04:33]
10. **Dissolve**: a gradual change directly into another shot. It suggests a longer passage of time than a cut would. [05:02]
11. A dissolve can also signal a memory or a dream. [05:37]
12. **Superimposition**: the mid-dissolve "third shot" with both images visible. Use it to compare two subjects or draw a parallel (Godfather II, Michael and Vito). [06:05–06:32]
13. **Match cut**: an instant transition in which elements of the previous shot match the next. The match can be audio or visual: shape, composition, colour, movement or sound. [06:32–07:01]
14. A match cut makes the viewer subconsciously compare similarities or differences between scenes. [07:34]
15. A match cut bridges time and space (2001). Match-cutting between faces implies the same person at two points in their life. [07:34]
16. One match cut can carry story and character beats at once (Pirates: same character, years later, same fascination). [08:02]
17. **Match dissolve** = match cut + dissolve. It keeps the match's function but smooths the abruptness (Toy Story 2: face into sun). [08:02–08:33]
18. **Iris** is outdated as an opener or closer. Modern use is stylistic: it focuses the whole scene on one element or idea, like a zoom-in. An opening iris can show a world opening up (The Departed). [09:07–10:02]
19. **Wipe**: shot B replaces shot A moving in from a direction (up, down, left, right, diagonal), or in shapes such as a star or clock. [10:31–11:31]
20. Wipes and irises look old-fashioned. Use them for a vintage look; today they are mostly used ironically or for comedy. [11:31–12:01]
21. **Pass-by / passing transition**: a moving person or object crosses the frame, or the camera moves past an object, to carry into the next scene. Pair it with "strategic sound design" so the momentum "never skips a beat" (Edgar Wright). [12:01–12:32]
22. A pass-by is more seamless than a wipe. Use it to hide a cut or to add a hyper-stylised look. [13:01]
23. **Whip pan / swish pan**: a fast horizontal rotation that blurs the image, used mid-scene or as a transition to *sustain momentum* from the previous scene. [13:30]
24. Whip-pan chains can express a hectic or dizzy mental state, which plain cuts would not (Boogie Nights). [13:30–14:03]
25. **Smash cut**: an abrupt cut with high contrast in **both** visuals and audio, such as quiet → loud or chaotic → still. [14:03–15:00]
26. Use smash cuts to shock or jolt (startling awake from a dream) or for comedy, punctuating a moment by contrast. [15:00–15:30]
27. **J-cut (pre-lap)**: the next scene's audio comes before its image. Use it in dialogue and to bridge into a flashback or memory (waves before Normandy in Saving Private Ryan). [16:03–17:03]
28. **L-cut**: the previous scene's audio carries over into the next scene. It is often used for narration. [17:03]
29. Sound A over image B creates a "more complicated and nuanced meaning" than either alone. [17:32]
30. Other transitions exist (push, zoom, luma key, barrel roll). Options are endless, but they are chosen for function. [18:01]
31. A transition's job is to create momentum, make connections and keep the viewer engaged. [18:31]

**Count: 31 rules.**

### 2.4 What is a Jump Cut & When to Use It: 5 Essential Jump Cut Techniques

- **Source:** 2.4 · StudioBinder · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=03zw4RNv0gE · 818 s · 1,585 words · direct match.
- **Thesis:** A jump cut is a deliberate tool for style, energy, emotion, montage and (as the axial cut) emphasis. When it is unwanted, avoid it with the 30° rule or a significant change in shot size.

**Rules**
1. **Traditional jump cut**: remove a segment from the middle of one shot and splice the ends together, so the shot jumps in time. [00:31–01:02]
2. A jump-cut effect can also come from cutting two *separate* shots of similar size and composition (Eternal Sunshine). [01:02]
3. Origins: Méliès's camera jam, then the 1920s Soviet filmmakers and Godard's French New Wave, which embraced discontinuity. Jump cuts read as disruptive and expressionist. [02:02–02:30]
4. **Style**: use jump cuts for a kinetic, stylish opening (Snatch). [03:00]
5. Jump cuts fit poetic, impressionistic or memory-based storytelling better than logic-driven plot (The Tree of Life). [03:30]
6. **Energy**: jumping through time speeds up an already energetic moment, or makes a simple moment intriguing. [04:03]
7. Time jump cuts to "a booming rhythm in the score". A throwaway transition (a plane landing) then builds anticipation and momentum. [04:31]
8. Use jump cuts in montage to show adrenalised energy (Run Lola Run). [05:02]
9. **Emotion**: jump cuts put the viewer inside a character's mental state, usually frantic, panicked or stressed. [05:30]
10. A jump cut combined with a sudden jolt in the sound reads as a break back to reality. [05:30]
11. Removing only "a couple of frames" from an action gives it an extra push of force (L.A. Confidential, breaking the chair). [06:04–06:32]
12. Micro jump cuts of "fractions of a second" add frantic energy and may go unnoticed by untrained eyes (The Departed, packing). [07:13]
13. Keep a jump cut when it plays. Don't "fix" an accidental one that works (The Irishman: "just leave it as a jump cut"). [07:31]
14. **Montage**: its main job is to compress time, so jump cuts fit naturally, including in training montages. [07:31]
15. Jump cuts can share a character's childlike exuberance (Bottle Rocket). [08:00]
16. A single locked camera angle plus jump cuts plus body language can tell a whole sequence (Schindler's List secretary interviews). [08:30–09:22]
17. **Axial cut**: a jump-cut subtype that punches in or out along the same axis. It works like a zoom, emphasising something important. [09:32]
18. Use an axial cut to show a jarring realisation (Catch Me If You Can). [10:06]
19. Back-and-forth axial cuts give frenzied energy (Trainspotting interview). [10:36]
20. Axial cuts heighten horror or supernatural power (The Ring). Two successive axial cuts amplify shock (Hitchcock, The Birds). [11:01–11:31]
21. To avoid an *unwanted* jump cut, follow the 30° rule: at the same shot size, the angle difference between shot A and shot B should be at least 30°. Less than that looks like a jump cut. [12:00]
22. Alternatively, keep the angle but change the shot size *significantly*. Wide → close-up feels natural; two slightly different wides do not. [12:30]

**Count: 22 rules.**

### 2.5 Rhythm in Film Editing (replacement for "What is RHYTHM in video editing and how can it help you create BETTER VIDEOS")

- **Source:** 2.5 · "Rhythm in Film Editing" · John Tindell · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WOVroZM6NCo · 597 s (9:57) · 824 words · manual replacement (match 1.0). The curriculum named a *This Guy Edits* video; the first resolved link was an InVideo video with garbled machine captions and was rejected. This replacement is on topic and the captions are clean. About 4 minutes of the runtime ([04:01–07:09]) is the Psycho shower scene played with a bell on every edit, so the transcript there holds only sound tags.
- **Thesis:** Rhythm is the pacing of the edits, measured as shot duration. An editor sets a baseline average shot length, then speeds it up to build tension and slows it down to create calm or release tension. The Psycho shower scene shows this in three measurable phases: build, attack, release.

**Rules**
1. Define editing rhythm as **the pacing of the edits**, that is, how long each shot stays on screen before the next cut. [00:05]
2. Rhythm is most obvious when the cuts are synced to music, as in the Suicide Squad trailer. [00:05]
3. Use cut-to-music syncing mainly for **trailers, musicals and montages**. In ordinary scenes, rhythm is usually less obvious. [00:31]
4. Treat rhythm as an emotional and psychological lever even when nobody notices it. "Less obvious" rhythm still has a "huge impact" on how viewers engage with a scene. [00:31–01:03]
5. Use rhythm for one of two jobs: **create tension** or **infuse calm**. [01:03]
6. Baseline: in most films the **average shot duration is 4–6 seconds**. [01:03]
7. **Establish an average shot duration first, then change the rhythm by shortening or lengthening shots relative to it.** The baseline is what makes a change readable. [02:00]
8. **Fast cutting = tension.** [02:38]
9. Speed the rhythm up for a chase, an angry argument or a violent encounter. [03:02]
10. **Slow cutting = calm.** Slow it down for a romantic encounter, a tender moment or a sad occasion. [03:02]
11. Study the Psycho shower scene as the benchmark for rhythm (the documentary *78/52* covers it). A big part of its reputation comes from how it handles rhythm. [03:33]
12. **Make rhythm visible to analyse it.** Overlay a marker for each edit on the scene, or put a sound (a bell) on every cut. This shows exactly where shot duration speeds up and slows down. [04:01, 07:09]
13. Marking each edit on a timeline lets you *see* the rhythm. A sound on each edit lets you hear it, which is how we normally experience rhythm. [07:09]
14. Read a sequence's rhythm in **three sections: before the peak, the peak itself, and after the peak.** In Psycho these are before, during and after the attack. [07:09–07:30]
15. Build section, as measured: **13 edits in 72 s, average shot duration 5.5 s.** [07:30]
16. **Put the longest hold right before the peak.** The longest shot of the build (**18 s**, about 3.3 times the section average) comes just before the attack. It works like the music going silent before a jump scare: "the calm setting up the storm". [07:30–08:02]
17. Peak section, as measured: **31 edits in 22 s, average shot duration just under 0.75 s.** That is about 7.7 times faster than the build. (The ratio is my arithmetic; both averages are the video's.) [08:02]
18. **Match the cutting to the violence of the content.** "The editing in this section is brutal", just like the attack. [08:02]
19. Release section, as measured: **8 edits in 67 s, average shot duration 8.4 s.** That is slower than the build baseline (5.5 s), not just a return to it. [08:31]
20. **After the peak, lengthen the shots to release tension and restore calm.** [08:31]
21. The sound follows the same three phases. The caption tags show soft music and running water in the build, high-pitched dramatic music and screaming in the attack, then running water alone after it. (Read from the bracketed sound tags, not from narration.) [04:01–06:33]
22. Fast-for-tension and slow-for-calm is only the starting point. Rhythm in editing goes beyond that. [09:01]
23. **Train your sense of rhythm.** Watch films and clap on every edit to feel the cutting pattern. [09:01–09:32]

**Count: 23 rules.** The video gives no frame counts or tempo figures. Its numbers are the 4–6 s film baseline and the three Psycho section averages (5.5 s / just under 0.75 s / 8.4 s), plus the 18 s hold before the peak.

### 2.6 How to Pace Your Film: Examples of Good and Bad Pacing in Editing, Writing and More

- **Source:** 2.6 · StudioBinder · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a9reuDw4cnU · 1,023 s · 1,803 words · direct match.
- **Thesis:** Pacing is the speed at which a story is told. It should vary (speed up, slow down, speed up). Script, coverage, camera, performance, cut rate and music all set it, and the edit is where it is finally fixed.

**Rules**
1. Pacing = the speed at which the story is told. Don't run at a single pace. Like McKee's long-distance runner, "speeds, slows, then speeds again" in cycles. [00:30–01:02]
2. Cut rate and story pace are separate. Early cinema used long takes (slow editing) in short runtimes (fast stories). [01:02]
3. Use pacing to steer emotion (Griffith's cross-cutting) and to heighten suspense and shock (Hitchcock). [01:33]
4. Pace works at the micro level (scene) and the macro level (sequence, whole film). Check both. [01:33–02:04]
5. The script is the first place pace is set. [02:32]
6. A script can state tempo changes outright (Tár: "there will be tempo changes"). [02:32–03:03]
7. Writing action in short bursts broken by ellipses and line breaks builds tension and emphasises scares (A Quiet Place). [03:03]
8. A steady, meditative pace lets small moments breathe (Past Lives). [03:52]
9. Direction can explicitly lengthen the pause between lines ("they just look at each other for a moment"). [04:00–04:30]
10. Rapid-fire dialogue with no action lines creates its own rhythm (Sorkin). [04:30–05:00]
11. Scene headings like "30 minutes later" / "soon after" signal breakneck, location-hopping pace (Uncut Gems). [05:35]
12. Having a character juggle several conversations at once creates urgency. [06:02]
13. Short, fragmented scenes (most under a page) show lives spiralling out of control (Requiem for a Dream). [06:31]
14. Covering a scene in only one or two shots tends to slow its tempo. [07:01]
15. Long single takes build a dreamlike, meditative quality (Apichatpong). [07:36]
16. A single take can still be fast if the camera is handheld and nearly always moving (Children of Men). [08:05]
17. Crash zooms and whip pans create fast pacing (Kill Bill). [08:33]
18. Locked-off shots with almost no camera movement let scenes breathe (Ozu). [08:33–09:00]
19. A slow, sedate dolly can evoke a dream state; a moving camera need not be fast (Stalker). [09:00]
20. Performers slow down to show inner emotion; silences carry feeling (Nomadland). [10:03]
21. A long beat or dramatic pause can land comedy; follow it with fast dialogue to ramp the pace (Grand Budapest). [10:30–11:01]
22. "The art of editing is the art of pacing." How long you stay on a shot or scene directly sets the story's speed. [11:32]
23. Fast cuts are the most immediate way to raise tempo (Baz Luhrmann). [12:02]
24. Sparse cutting and lingering shots let viewers sink into a world (Chantal Akerman). [12:02–12:34]
25. Most work sits between the extremes. [12:34]
26. Change tempo inside a sequence: leisurely cuts, then a burst of quick cuts at the inciting event (Hurt Locker, gunshot). [13:01]
27. Or the reverse: frequent cuts, then holding on slow motion to stretch drama and tragedy (Platoon). [13:30]
28. Fast edit plus propulsive narration gives an extremely fast sequence (The Rules of Attraction). [14:02]
29. Fewer cuts can cover *more* narrative if the narration is dense (The Wolf of Wall Street). Information rate is not the same as cut rate. [14:02]
30. Music has a large effect on pace. Slow music under a suspense scene removes the suspense; an accelerating score braces the viewer for a scare (Jaws). [14:32–15:12]
31. A ticking-clock element in the score adds urgency even to scenes that would not otherwise feel rushed (Dunkirk). [15:30]
32. If pacing isn't working, the edit is where it must be fixed. [15:30]
33. Pacing starts at a project's inception; plan it in script, storyboard and shot list. [16:09]

**Count: 33 rules.**

### 2.7 Ultimate Guide to Diegetic vs Non-Diegetic Sound: Definitions, Examples & How to Break the Rules

- **Source:** 2.7 (also listed as 5.3) · StudioBinder · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2vlwm4VyyTc · 1,047 s · 1,839 words · search match 0.83, confirmed correct title and channel.
- **Thesis:** Sound is either inside the story world (diegetic) or heard only by the audience (non-diegetic). Each builds the world or the emotion, and the most expressive moments come from breaking or switching between the two.

**Rules**
1. Most sound design is meant to go unnoticed, but it is the "unsung hero". Plan sound as seriously as picture. [00:00]
2. **Diegetic** = anything the characters can experience inside the story world. **Non-diegetic** = what only the audience perceives. [00:30–01:01]
3. Non-diegetic elements can be visual too: title cards, captions, inserts from outside the story world. [01:01–01:31]
4. Chion's three zones: (a) off-screen diegetic sound whose source isn't visible (acousmatic, e.g. unseen birds); (b) non-diegetic sound characters can't hear (acousmatic, e.g. score); (c) the visualised zone, whose source is on screen. Every sound sits in one zone and can move between zones. [02:04–02:31]
5. Diegetic sound includes atmosphere, weather, vehicles, weapons, source music, dialogue and some voice-over. [03:00]
6. A voice-over of a character's thoughts is **internal diegetic** sound: part of the story world even though other characters can't hear it. [03:30]
7. The main job of diegetic sound is to establish and build the world around the characters. [03:30]
8. Off-screen sound tells the viewer about the setting and expands the world beyond the frame. Withholding the source creates suspense. [04:01–04:36]
9. Break the expectation for impact: total **silence** where a huge sound is expected (The Last Jedi). [04:36–05:01]
10. Contrapuntal sound: put a sound against the action (cheerful song over violence) for disturbing juxtaposition (A Clockwork Orange). [05:01]
11. Point of audition: shape sound so the viewer hears what the character hears, the audio version of a POV shot. [05:01]
12. Distort or alter sound to convey a compromised mental state. [05:30]
13. **Non-diegetic** covers added sound effects, musical score and narration. [06:32]
14. Narration from a narrator who plays no role in the story is non-diegetic and recalls traditional verbal storytelling (Royal Tenenbaums). [07:01]
15. Risk: a recognisable narrator voice (the director's) can break the illusion and the fourth wall. Do it only on purpose (Hateful Eight). [07:30–08:01]
16. Non-diegetic sound effects strengthen motion and movement, raising intensity. [08:01]
17. Use a sound effect to put a punchline on a joke. [08:31]
18. Score is the most consistently used non-diegetic sound. Audiences are used to it, and it carries emotion: transcendence, heartbreak, triumph (Interstellar, Up, Black Panther). [08:31–09:30]
19. **Trans-diegetic** sound switches between modes, and subverting expectations is where interesting storytelling happens. [09:30–10:01]
20. Score can turn out to be diegetic (it's on a radio, or a character asks for it to stop), or diegetic music can become non-diegetic over a montage "to help smooth over the time jumps". [10:01–11:08]
21. A sound effect can carry across a scene change: diegetic wind becomes non-diegetic and bridges, even comically (Raising Arizona). [11:08–11:31]
22. Assumed non-diegetic narration can be revealed as diegetic (the Stranger appears in The Big Lebowski). [11:31]
23. Switching modes can blur fantasy and reality. Revealing the "score" drummer on screen shows an unstable mind (Birdman). [12:02]
24. A total breakdown, where the narrator argues with characters, works as comedy (George of the Jungle). [12:30]
25. Edge cases that resist classification: a narrator speaking from beyond death, narration that turns out to be testimony, internal monologue in another character's voice, a character singing along to "score" that is in their head, background music becoming internal music, an ensemble singing a non-diegetic song. Use them deliberately for ambiguity. [13:01–16:02]
26. Sound is as useful as cinematography and editing for storytelling. Plan sound in pre-production. [16:34–17:00]

### 2.8 The Editing That Makes Your Story 10x Deeper (Hayden Hillier-Smith)

- **Source:** 2.8 · HillierSmith · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mPhhBgTIG2Y · 880 s · 2,629 words · direct match. (Includes a Logitech sponsor segment at [09:01–10:00], which teaches nothing.)
- **Thesis:** Perspective editing means anchoring every shot and cut to the protagonist's viewpoint and reactions. Built on a Dunning–Kruger arc (overconfidence → humbled → growth), it turns a flat "goal → achieved" edit into a story with depth, and it is the "human touch" missing from edits that feel AI-made.

**Rules**
1. Perspective editing separates "a mediocre edit that feels like it was made with AI" from one with a human touch. [00:00]
2. Put the protagonist's reaction shots (just looking out) between grand or landscape shots, so the viewer feels the scale *through* the person (Kuleshov). [00:00–00:31]
3. Go beyond the basic Kuleshov effect and apply perspective to the whole story structure. [00:31]
4. V1 critique, eye trace: "not tracing eye lines". Give the object of action (the Rubik's cube) **centre frame** so the viewer finds it at once. [03:00]
5. Show multiple instances to create scale. When the voice says "a few tutorials", show three: "one, two, three". [03:00–03:30]
6. Don't undermine the story. A straight "goal → journey → achieved" is simple and boring; develop conflict. [03:30–04:00]
7. Use obstacles that block the goal, and especially ways the character blocks himself. [04:00]
8. Make each shot and cut relate back to the protagonist's perspective, "see the world through his eyes". [04:30]
9. Look for reasons to cut back to the protagonist's face and reaction. For every piece of information ask what it means to the protagonist. [04:30]
10. For each beat ask how to represent the emotion, the tone and the story at that point. This is the hardest part of editing. [04:30–05:00]
11. Step back to see the macro structure of the story before deciding a beat. [05:00]
12. Use the **Dunning–Kruger narrative**: Peak of Mount Stupid (high confidence, low competence) → Valley of Despair (awareness of complexity; where most quit) → Slope of Enlightenment (practical learning) → sustainable confidence. [05:00–06:31]
13. Open by climbing Mount Stupid: tease the hook, then build stakes with perspective (the protagonist looking round at the competition) instead of shots from no one's point of view. [07:00–07:31]
14. Build contrast: show the protagonist expert in one domain, then amateur in this one ("here's you being professional, here's you being amateur"). [07:31–08:00]
15. Favour footage that shows the protagonist arrogant or not taking it seriously, and **if you don't have it, film it** (pickups). [08:00]
16. Literalise the motivation on screen: show the protagonist watching the inspiring thing (on TV) rather than just saying it. [08:00–08:31]
17. Music sets the tone and characterisation. The same line reads as "high society" or "badassery" depending on the track. [08:31]
18. Reshoot to fix story logic. If a reveal's prop is already in shot, the "loop" is already closed. Hide it (under objects, blow dust off it) so the prop tells the story (forgotten, neglected). [10:00–10:31]
19. Introduce an expert by putting him "on a pedestal" first: three textural shots of him doing impressive things, then the meeting. [10:31–11:01]
20. Double down on humbling the protagonist. Find the expert's reaction beats (head shake, sigh, laugh, eye-roll) and cut to them. [11:01–11:31]
21. "Have your protagonist fail early". Watching someone grow is engaging. [11:31]
22. The golden rule of web media: immediately deliver the video's promise to earn trust and attention (a "proven retention tactic"). [12:32]
23. Then stack quick, satisfying short loops to hold attention to the end. [12:32]
24. That formula works but is forgettable. Improve it: show the goal and the attempt, but have it "shyly" miss; withholding the gap's closure creates pull. [13:00–13:30]
25. Overconfidence quickly humbled motivates a story of change: you deliver the promise *and* promise a transformation. [13:30–14:01]
26. State the goal early: once viewers know it, they want to see the loop closed. [14:01]

**Count: 26 rules.**

## B. Module synthesis

### B.1 Best practices (de-duplicated, ordered by impact)

UNIVERSAL = applies to every edit. CONTEXT = applies only when the stated condition holds.

1. **Rank every cut decision Emotion > Story > Rhythm > Eye trace > Planarity > 3D continuity. When forced to trade, give up the bottom ones; higher-order wins hide lower-order flaws, not the reverse.** UNIVERSAL. [2.1 r15–r23, 2.2 r4]
2. **Cut on the completed thought.** The cut point is the end of an idea (sentence or clause end, a blink, a settle), not a mechanical "after every line". Cut too early and it rushes; too late and it drags. UNIVERSAL. [2.1 r4–r9, r17]
3. **Every cut must be purposeful: decide what to show, when to cut and what to cut to.** UNIVERSAL. [2.2 r2, r9; 2.3 r31]
4. **Anchor the edit to the protagonist's perspective: keep finding reasons to return to the face and reaction, and ask what each beat means to them.** UNIVERSAL for person-led video. [2.8 r2, r8–r10; 2.2 r10–r12]
5. **Vary the pace in cycles (fast, slow, fast). Speed up with shorter shots and denser narration; slow down with holds and silence. Don't run at one tempo. Set a baseline average shot length first, then deviate from it: shorter shots for tension, longer shots for calm.** UNIVERSAL. [2.6 r1, r22–r27; 2.2 r5–r6; 2.5 r5–r10]
5b. **Shape each peak as build → burst → release. Hold the longest shot of the build right before the burst ("the calm setting up the storm"). Cut the burst far faster than the baseline. After it, cut slower than the baseline to release the tension.** Psycho: 5.5 s → 0.71 s → 8.4 s average shot length, with an 18 s hold just before the attack. UNIVERSAL (the ratios, not the film's absolute seconds). [2.5 r14–r20]
6. **Avoid near-identical consecutive framings (the bee-hive problem). Either change angle by ≥30° or change shot size significantly, or make the jump deliberate (axial punch-in for emphasis).** UNIVERSAL. [2.1 r12–r13, 2.4 r17, r21–r22]
7. **Use split edits (J/L): let the voice lead or trail the picture so cuts feel natural and you can show what is being described.** UNIVERSAL. [2.2 r22–r27, 2.3 r27–r29]
8. **Keep eye trace: put the incoming shot's focus where the eye already is. In fast cutting (≈1.8 s or less per shot), centre the subject.** UNIVERSAL, more important the faster you cut. [2.1 r19–r20, 2.2 r19–r21, 2.8 r4]
9. **Deliver the promise immediately, state the goal early, then hold attention with short satisfying loops, while deliberately delaying the final payoff.** CONTEXT: web and short-form. [2.8 r22–r26]
10. **Show, don't just tell: when the voice names something (a plan, a list, "three tutorials"), cut to it, matching the count.** UNIVERSAL. [2.2 r26, 2.8 r5, r16]
11. **Choose transitions for their meaning: a cut by default; fade = closure or chapter; dissolve = time or memory; match cut = connection; whip or pass-by = momentum; smash = jolt; wipe or iris = vintage or ironic.** UNIVERSAL. [2.1 r32, 2.3 r2–r31]
12. **Music sets pace and tone. Its tempo and character must fit the scene: slow music kills urgency, a ticking pulse adds urgency, and the track choice changes how the same line reads.** UNIVERSAL. [2.6 r30–r31, 2.8 r17, 2.7 r18]
13. **Use sound effects (non-diegetic) to strengthen motion and land punchlines; time jump cuts and cut points to strong beats in the score.** CONTEXT: stylised and energetic pieces. Cutting in sync with music is mainly a trailer, musical and montage device, not the default for dialogue. [2.7 r16–r17, 2.4 r7, 2.5 r2–r3]
14. **Cut on action: match shot A's out-point to shot B's in-point mid-movement; cut on impact for force.** CONTEXT: footage with movement. [2.2 r32–r36]
15. **Use jump cuts on purpose for energy, emotion, montage (time compression) and emphasis (axial punch). Removing a few frames adds force; leave a jump that plays.** CONTEXT: when the style allows visible jumps. [2.4 r4–r20]
16. **Silence and contrast are tools. Silence where sound is expected, a smash cut on a loudness contrast, a held pause before a fast run.** CONTEXT. [2.7 r9, 2.3 r25–r26, 2.6 r21, 2.5 r16]
17. **Juxtaposition makes meaning (Kuleshov, intellectual montage, cross-cutting, sound A + image B). Pairing is the edit's main way to say something the words don't.** UNIVERSAL. [2.1 r3, r11; 2.2 r16–r18, r28–r31; 2.3 r29; 2.8 r2]
18. **Build conflict: overconfidence → humbled → growth. Fail early, use contrast (expert vs amateur) and set up an expert before introducing them.** CONTEXT: narrative and creator content. [2.8 r6–r7, r12–r21]
19. **Judge only what is on screen, as a first-time viewer; when viewers dislike a section, look upstream for missing information first.** UNIVERSAL. [2.1 r29–r31]
20. **Keep the 180° line and spatial continuity, but rank them last.** CONTEXT: multi-camera or multi-person scenes. [2.1 r21–r22, r14; 2.2 r13]
21. **Plan pace and sound before the shoot, and get pickups when the footage can't tell the story.** UNIVERSAL. [2.1 r25, r33; 2.6 r5, r33; 2.7 r26; 2.8 r15, r18]
22. **Mind the narrator's voice: an unexpected or recognisable outside voice breaks immersion. Keep the voice consistent unless you are breaking it on purpose.** CONTEXT. [2.7 r14–r15]
23. **Off-screen and ambient sound expand the world beyond the frame.** CONTEXT: where the location matters. [2.7 r5, r7–r8]
24. **Pick a representative still per take (a contact sheet) to speed up take selection.** UNIVERSAL workflow. [2.1 r26]
25. **Audit rhythm by making it visible or audible: mark every edit on a timeline or put a click on every cut, then read the pattern.** UNIVERSAL workflow. [2.5 r12–r13, r23]
26. **Match cut intensity to content intensity: brutal action gets brutal cutting, tender or sad moments get long holds.** UNIVERSAL. [2.5 r8–r10, r18]

| 23 | Ambient and off-screen sound | Room tone and ambience should be cleaned, not featured (podcast-mic spec). A short, subtle ambient bed under client B-roll (shop sounds) can help, under the voice. | PARTIAL |

Rules that do NOT transfer:
- Feature-film continuity (180°, 3D continuity, shot/reverse shot), because there is one camera and one speaker.
- Fades and dissolves as time markers (an ad has no time jumps, and a fade stalls the feed).
- Wipes and irises (they read as dated or comedic, against a premium designed look).
- Cross-cutting parallel storylines (no parallel scenes).
- Long single-take or meditative pacing (Ozu, Akerman), because there is no time in 30–60 s.
- Visible stylistic jump cuts (EDIT-STANDARD requires hidden cuts).
- Trans-diegetic tricks and narrator ambiguity (too clever for a direct-response ad).
- Murch's "edit standing", which is a human-ergonomics tip.

### B.3 Checklist

**Pre-edit**
- [ ] Transcript with word timings exists (whisper.cpp) and has been read end to end.
- [ ] One representative still per take or segment is on a contact sheet. [2.1 r26]
- [ ] The ad's emotion is named in one line (e.g. "relief: someone will handle this for me"). [2.1 r15]
- [ ] The story beats are mapped onto the transcript: hook/promise, problem, proof/list, offer, CTA. [2.8 r22, 2.1 r16]
- [ ] The hook (promise or pain) is within the first sentence of the cut. [2.8 r22]
- [ ] The goal or promise is stated early enough that the CTA "closes the loop". [2.8 r26]
- [ ] A pace curve is sketched (which beats are fast, which breathe). [2.6 r1]
- [ ] Every planned cutaway is tied to specific spoken words. [2.2 r9, 2.8 r5]
- [ ] Missing visuals or lines have been flagged for pickups rather than faked. [2.8 r15, r18]
- [ ] A music track is chosen for tempo and character to match the delivery. [2.6 r30, 2.8 r17]

**During edit**
- [ ] Every A-roll cut is on a word boundary at a clause or sentence end (completed thought). [2.1 r4]
- [ ] No two consecutive A-roll shots share the same framing without a cutaway between them. [2.1 r12, 2.4 r22]
- [ ] Any punch-in for *emphasis* lands on the key word. [2.4 r17]
- [ ] The voice continues uninterrupted across every cutaway in and out (L/J). [2.2 r22–r25]
- [ ] Each cutaway's focal element sits near the outgoing face position, or at centre. [2.1 r19, 2.2 r21]
- [ ] Each cutaway shows what is being said, at the moment it is said, with item counts matching the words. [2.8 r5]
- [ ] Every transition has a reason (momentum → whip; emphasis → punch; default → cut). No dissolves, fades, wipes or irises mid-ad. [2.3]
- [ ] The edit returns to the face after cutaways often enough that the speaker remains the anchor. [2.8 r9]
- [ ] Pace varies: at least one faster run (list or proof) and one beat that breathes (the CTA). [2.6 r26–r27]
- [ ] The ad's baseline average shot length is known (from the cut list), and each section deliberately sits above or below it. [2.5 r7]
- [ ] The shot right before the peak run is the longest hold in that section, the peak run cuts faster than the baseline, and the CTA cuts slower than the baseline (build → burst → release). [2.5 r16–r20]
- [ ] Cuts are synced to music beats only in a montage-like run, not across the whole ad. [2.5 r3]
- [ ] SFX are placed on visual motion events (whips, pops, punch-ins), not on empty frames. [2.7 r16]
- [ ] The music character changes or lifts at the offer, if the cut supports it. [2.6 r30]

**Pre-delivery**
- [ ] Watched once start to finish on a phone, sound on, as a first-time viewer. [2.1 r29]
- [ ] Watched once sound off: the story still reads from picture and captions. [2.7 r3]
- [ ] No cut feels early (clipped thought) or late (dead air). [2.1 r17]
- [ ] No cut "jumps" between near-identical frames. [2.1 r12]
- [ ] The CTA is complete and unhurried. [2.6 r21]
- [ ] A click-on-every-cut review render has been heard once, and the rhythm shape (build → burst → release) is audible. [2.5 r12–r13, r23]
- [ ] All automated gates (B.5 plus EDIT-STANDARD §4) pass.

| Does it feel human-made rather than "AI-made"? | Cut choices respond to meaning and reactions, not a fixed template or interval. | 2.8 r1 |

### B.5 QC (automatable gates)

Thresholds marked **(house)** come from EDIT-STANDARD; **(video)** come from the transcripts; **(industry norm)** are named norms; **(calibrate)** means the videos give no number, so measure the user's approved `Inspirations/` edits and set the threshold from them. Don't invent one.

| # | Gate | Metric | Pass | How to measure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | Cut on thought boundary | For each cut (A-roll ↔ A-roll and A-roll ↔ cutaway exit), the distance from the cut frame to the nearest whisper word end, and whether that word ends a clause (punctuation or pause ≥ breath gap) | 100% of cuts fall between words (never inside a word's [start, end]); ≥ (calibrate)% of them at clause or sentence ends | Cut list (edl_v2.json) × whisper.cpp word timings; punctuation from the transcript |
| Q2 | No near-identical framing (bee-hive / jump cut) | Across each A-roll→A-roll cut: YuNet face-box height ratio, plus SSIM of downscaled frames on either side | Scale ratio ≥ 1.15 **(house)** OR the cut is covered by a cutaway; flag any SSIM above (calibrate) with scale ratio < 1.15 | YuNet boxes on the last frame before and first frame after the cut; skimage SSIM at 1/4 resolution |
| Q3 | Voice continuity across cutaways (L/J) | Voice-stem VAD state at each cutaway in and out frame, and whether the picture cut sits on a word boundary | The voice never drops to silence *because of* a picture cut; no word is split by an audio edit at a picture cut | VAD or RMS on the voice stem ±3 frames around each cut; whisper word spans |
| Q4 | Eye trace across cuts | Normalised distance between the outgoing focal point (YuNet face centre) and the incoming focal point (bbox centre of the cutaway's primary element), and the reverse on return | Report distance per cut; flag above (calibrate). Distances in the upper 9:16 band and near centre are preferred for shots ≤ ~1.8 s **(video: 2.2 Fury Road)** | YuNet face centre; graphics layer bboxes from the gfx.py render manifest |
| Q5 | Pace variation | Shot-length series from the cut list: mean (the ad's baseline average shot length), coefficient of variation, and the mean per beat section | Report. Absolute film seconds (4–6 s baseline) do not apply; calibrate the ratios on Inspirations. | Cut list durations; beat sections from the transcript map |
| Q5b | Build → burst → release | (a) Mean shot length of the peak section ÷ ad baseline; (b) mean shot length of the post-peak (offer/CTA) section ÷ ad baseline; (c) whether the shot immediately before the peak section is the longest in its section | (a) < 1 and (b) > 1 **(video 2.5: fast peak, slower-than-baseline release)**; (c) true, or flagged for review **(video 2.5: 18 s hold right before the attack)**; exact ratios (calibrate) | Cut list (edl_v2.json) durations, counting cutaway in/out and in-cutaway item changes as "edits"; section boundaries from the beat map |
| Q5c | Click-track review render | A review-only audio track with a click at every cut frame | Produced for every review render (pass = it exists and has been heard); not a numeric gate **(video 2.5: bells on each edit)** | ffmpeg: mix short click samples at cut timestamps from the cut list into a review copy; never into the delivery file |
| Q6 | Promise in the hook | Time of the first word of the promise or pain sentence | Hook sentence starts at 0 s and the promise is stated within the first ~3 s **(industry norm for Reels/Meta: first 3 s decide the scroll; video 2.8 says "immediately")** | whisper word timings vs the beat map |

| Q8 | Transition whitelist | Detect crossfades or dissolves (gradual frame-difference ramp over > 2 frames), fades to black (mean luma < ~16 for ≥ 2 frames) and wipes | Zero dissolves, fades or wipes inside the ad body; an optional fade-out only in the final ≤ 1 s | Per-frame mean luma and frame-difference curve (ffmpeg signalstats / Python) |
| Q9 | SFX tied to motion | Every SFX onset vs the nearest visual event (whip, punch-in, element appear) | Each SFX onset within ±1 frame of an event **(house: "on the frame")**; zero orphan SFX | SFX placement list from mix_v2.py vs render event list; confirm with librosa onset detection on the SFX stem |
| Q10 | Beat alignment (optional) | Fraction of cutaway entries within ±2 frames of a librosa beat or downbeat | Report only, (calibrate). Speech-boundary timing (Q1) overrides beat timing (Rule of Six: story > rhythm). ### B.6 Anti-patterns and failure modes

| Anti-pattern | Symptom | Fix | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mid-thought cut | The speaker is cut off before a clause ends; feels rushed or clipped | Move the cut to the word end at the clause boundary (Q1) | 2.1 r4, r17 |
| Dragging cut | A dead beat after a thought finishes before the cut or cutaway | Trim to the thought end plus a breath gap; tighten per the pause gate | 2.1 r17, 2.6 r22 |
| Bee-hive jump | Two near-identical framings back to back; the face "twitches" | Punch in ≥ 1.15× or cover with a cutaway (Q2) | 2.1 r12, 2.4 r21–r22 |
| Dragnet metronome | A cut every line at an even interval; feels mechanical or "AI" | Let meaning drive cut timing; vary shot length per beat (Q5) | 2.1 r9, 2.8 r1 |
| Decorative cutaway | A graphic that doesn't illustrate the words, or shows before they're said | Tie every element to a spoken keyword (Q7) | 2.2 r9, 2.8 r5 |
| Lost protagonist | Long runs of graphics and the face disappears; trust drops | Cap spans without the face (Q12); return to the face for the reaction | 2.8 r8–r9 |
| Eye-hunt cut | The viewer's eye jumps from the face (top centre) to a graphic in a corner | Place the focal element near the previous focus or centre (Q4) | 2.1 r19, 2.2 r19–r21, 2.8 r4 |
| Meaning-blind transition | Dissolves, fades, star wipes or random presets mid-ad | Hard cut default; whip only for momentum; punch only for emphasis (Q8) | 2.3 r10, r20, r31 |
| Mid-ad fade to black | Momentum stalls and the viewer scrolls | Remove it; fade (if any) only at the final frame | 2.3 r7 |
| Monotone pace | Same shot length throughout; no lift, no settle | Speed up the list or proof, let the CTA breathe | 2.6 r1, r26–r27, 2.5 r7 |
| Storm without the calm | The fastest run comes straight out of equally fast cutting, so it doesn't feel faster | Hold the shot before the run longer than anything else in that section (Q5b c) | 2.5 r16 |
| No release | The cutting stays at peak speed into the offer and CTA; the close feels breathless and salesy | Lengthen shots after the peak, above the ad baseline (Q5b b) | 2.5 r19–r20 |
| Trailer-cutting a talking head | Every cut snaps to the music beat, whatever the words are doing; feels like a promo reel, not a person talking | Cut on thought completion (Q1); keep beat-syncing for a montage-like run | 2.5 r3, 2.1 r15–r17 |
| Fast-cut sincerity | Rapid cuts over a tender or empathetic line; the emotion reads as hype | Hold longer on sincere beats | 2.5 r10, r18 |
| Mismatched music tempo | Music slower or calmer than the delivery and the ad feels slow; or frantic music under a sincere close | Pick the track for tempo and character; switch or lift at the offer | 2.6 r30, 2.8 r17 |
| Orphan SFX | Whooshes or pops without a visual event, or offset from it | Snap SFX to event frames (Q9) | 2.7 r16 |
| Buried promise | The hook opens with a greeting or context, and the promise comes late | Move the promise or pain line to the first sentence (Q6) | 2.8 r22 |
| No loop | The CTA doesn't answer the hook | Make the CTA restate or close the promise | 2.8 r26 |
| Fixing the wrong spot | Reworking a weak section repeatedly when the cause is missing earlier info | Check what the viewer needed to know before that point | 2.1 r31 |
| Shoot bias | The editor keeps a take because it was hard to get or "was great on set" | Judge only the screen | 2.1 r29 |
| Voice break | A different or altered voice (TTS patch, generative enhancement) breaks the illusion | Single natural voice; words gate | 2.7 r15, EDIT-STANDARD §3 |

### B.7 Conflicts and open questions

**Between videos**

1. **Visible vs hidden jump cuts.** 2.4 celebrates jump cuts (style, energy, emotion, montage) and says "leave it" when one plays (r13). 2.1 (r12–r13) and 2.4 (r21–r22) also give rules to *avoid* them. These don't contradict: it depends on intent. *Call:* hide them by default (the first test client's style did; a client whose inspirations use visible jumps can allow them); the axial punch-in is the one sanctioned visible jump, used for emphasis.
2. **Cutting per line.** 2.1 (r9) calls the "Dragnet system" an anti-pattern, while 2.6 (r10, Sorkin) praises rapid-fire rhythm and 2.2 (r6) says frantic scenes need rapid cuts. *Call:* rapid cutting is fine, but its timing must follow thought completion, not a fixed one-cut-per-line metre.
3. **Rhythm vs story priority with music.** 2.4 (r7) times cuts to the score's beat; 2.1 ranks story above rhythm; 2.5 (r3) says music-synced cutting is mostly for trailers, musicals and montages. *Call:* with a talking head, speech boundaries (story) override music beats. Snap to a beat only when a word boundary falls within ±2 frames of one (Q10 is report-only).
4. **Dissolve meaning.** 2.3 (r10) says a dissolve implies a longer passage of time; 2.7 (r20) uses music to smooth time jumps in montage. No conflict; both say time-compression devices signal elapsed time, and an ad has none.

**Between videos and EDIT-STANDARD.md**

5. **Punch-in scale (1.15×) vs "change shot size *significantly*" (2.4 r22) and the bee-hive principle (2.1 r12).** EDIT-STANDARD hides A-roll jump cuts with a ~1.15× punch-in on every second cut. 2.4 says wide → close-up works but two *slightly* different sizes do not, which suggests a small 1.15× change could itself read as a near-identical framing. The videos give no numeric ratio. *Call:* keep 1.15× as the house baseline (the user validated it in the inspirations) but add Q2's SSIM check. If a 1.15× cut still scores near-identical (same head position and background), cover it with a cutaway or use a bigger punch. **Open question for the user:** confirm 1.15× reads as a "different shot" on a phone, or allow a larger alternate (the house "one extreme punch-in" for emphasis already exists).
6. **Pause gate (≤ 0.25 s, breaths 0.08–0.15 s) vs deliberate beats.** 2.6 (r9, r20–r21) say pauses and holds carry emotion and comedy, 2.5 (r16) uses a long hold as the calm before the storm, and 2.1 cuts on the completed thought. The house gate removes every pause above 0.25 s. *Call:* the house gate stands for inside-speech gaps (the user asked for tight delivery, and feeds punish dead air). Allow a single **whitelisted intentional beat** (e.g. before the offer reveal) only if the user approves a gate exception marker in the EDL. **Open question.** 2.5's hold is a *picture* hold (a long shot), not an audio pause, so it can be done under continuous speech without touching the pause gate.
6b. **Film shot-length baseline (4–6 s, 2.5 r6) vs the house 2–4 s A-roll rhythm and 3–8 s cutaways.** *Call:* house wins on absolute values: a 30–60 s Reels ad is not a feature film. Take from 2.5 the **relative** method (baseline, then deliberate deviation, with the build → burst → release ratios in Q5b), not its seconds.
7. **Cutaway share 40–55% vs perspective editing (2.8 r8–r9).** Heavy cutaway time risks losing the face, where trust lives. *Call:* no direct conflict. Keep 40–55%, but enforce Q12 (no span without the face over 8 s, the house cutaway max) and prefer returning to the face for the reaction after each key claim.
8. **"Nothing appears before it's said" vs J-cuts.** 2.2 and 2.3 recommend J-cuts (audio leads picture), and the house forbids a *visual* lead. They agree: a J-cut is sound first, picture after. A *visual pre-lap* (graphic before the word) is what the house bans, and the videos never recommend it. No conflict.
9. **Transitions.** EDIT-STANDARD allows the zoom-blur whip (matches 2.3 r23: momentum), glitch lines between cutaways (not covered by any video; acceptable as a stylised pass-by/momentum device per 2.3 r22, if it has an SFX), and one extreme punch-in (matches 2.4 r17 axial emphasis). "Never a hard switch of world or lighting" agrees with 2.1's emotion-first rule. No conflict; the videos add that fades, dissolves, wipes and irises should not be used mid-ad.
10. **Music loudness (−7 LU under voice) vs "sound should go unnoticed" (2.7 r1).** The user explicitly wants a big music bed. 2.7's "unnoticed" refers to sound *design* serving the story, not quiet music, and 2.6 (r30) and 2.8 (r17) say music sets pace and tone. *Call:* house wins; frequency-split ducking keeps words clear.
11. **The Rule of Six vs the gate list.** Every EDIT-STANDARD gate measures lower-order or technical items (continuity, overlap, loudness). None measures emotion, story or rhythm, which Murch ranks highest. *Call:* add the B.4 QA questions as a mandatory human review step, and add Q1, Q5, Q6 and Q12 as automated proxies for story and rhythm.
12. **Captions as non-diegetic visuals (2.7 r3).** No conflict. EDIT-STANDARD's small sentence-case captions are a non-diegetic layer and should stay unobtrusive, consistent with "most design meant to go unnoticed".
