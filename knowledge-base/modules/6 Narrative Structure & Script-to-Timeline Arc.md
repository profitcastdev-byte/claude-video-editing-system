### 6.1 Documentary Storytelling: Master 3 Act Structure
- **Source:** 6.1 | Documentary Film Academy | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Y3DvzFSc4o | 765 s (12:45) | 2,317 words | direct match, local Whisper transcript.
- **Thesis:** Even a true story needs its elements chosen and revealed in a set order. A three-act structure with six plot points (opening, inciting incident, first act break, midpoint, second act break, climax/resolution) gives that order. Plan it before you shoot and adapt it in the edit.

**Rules**
1. Even with real people and events, choose which elements of the story matter most. [00:33]
2. Reveal those elements in a deliberate order, the one that makes the story emotionally compelling. [00:33]
3. Build the structure before filming. It focuses the shoot and the edit and saves time. [00:33]
4. Treat the structure as a guideline, not a formula ("no rules, only guidelines"). Adapt it as the story changes. [01:05]
5. Use three acts: setup, development, climax/resolution (beginning, middle, end). [00:00]
6. Place two plot points in each act. Act 1: opening and inciting incident. Act 2: first act break and midpoint. Act 3: second act break and climax/resolution. [01:34]
7. In the opening, introduce the topic and main character(s), establish the location, and get the audience emotionally invested so they want to know what happens next. [02:03]
8. Build that investment by showing the character doing what they love, and their key relationship. [02:30]
9. Raise the stakes at the start with a **cold opener / teaser**: show an intense moment from later in the film before the titles. [02:30–03:07]
10. Online, hook within the **first few seconds**, or viewers swipe to the next piece of content. [03:07]
11. Show the stakes as concretely as possible in the teaser (Man on Wire: the danger is clear and failure is likely). [03:33]
12. The **inciting incident** = the character's want/goal becomes clear **and** an obstacle gets in the way. Together they set the character off. [04:00]
13. The want can come from one concrete trigger (Philippe sees a newspaper photo of towers not yet built). [04:34]
14. The **first act break** (start of Act 2) is when the character actually sets off to reach the goal. [05:04]
15. In Act 2, build tension. The audience should wonder how the challenges can ever be resolved. [05:30]
16. Make Act 2 the largest act, **often double the length of Act 1 and Act 3** (≈25 / 50 / 25). [05:30]
17. Put the **midpoint** about **halfway**: an unexpected setback that deepens the stakes while the character heads toward the goal. [06:02]
18. A strong midpoint makes the character look inward, see a flaw they were blind to, and change course. [06:02]
19. When interviewing, ask about struggles and past failures. The director missed a previous failed attempt and lost a scene that would have raised the Act 2 stakes. [06:32–07:04]
20. The harder the character has to work to overcome the obstacles, the more satisfying the ending. [07:04]
21. Show setbacks as real reversals: the team fails, goes home, rethinks, keeps going. [07:33]
22. The **second act break** (start of Act 3): the goal is almost in reach, then it looks impossible. Stakes go "full throttle". The character makes a decision they can't take back ("do or die"). [08:04]
23. Show the strain on faces so the audience asks "are they going to make it?" [08:33]
24. Stack obstacles right up to the climax (dawn breaks, the wire is still slack, they will be caught). [09:01]
25. The **climax** is the release: the goal is reached, with visible relief and celebration. [09:32–10:34]
26. The **resolution** ties up loose ends and resolves the conflict between what the character wants and what they need. [10:34]
27. In the resolution, answer the question the audience has been asking all film ("why did you do it?"). [11:05]
28. If you are stuck, **work backwards from Act 3 to Act 1**. Knowing the ending tells you how to set it up. [12:00]
29. Expect the structure to change again in the edit as you find new ways to tell the story. [12:00]

### 6.2 video editors NEED to improve their storytelling…
- **Source:** 6.2 | Colleen Edits (Colleen Cavolo) | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tu-qgwpQtCE | 625 s (10:25) | 2,023 words | search match, local Whisper. Self-promotion at [05:05] and [10:05]; no technique there.
- **Thesis:** Editors are the last writers. Know the basic narrative structure, keep the viewer focused with pacing, think like an ordinary viewer, cut beautiful but pointless material, treat sound as story, and get outside eyes.

**Rules**
1. Take in stories regularly (films, TV, podcasts while editing) to keep your idea flow up. [00:32–01:33]
2. Know the standard linear structure: introduction, inciting incident, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution, conclusion. [02:01]
3. Expect even good writers and directors to get the structure muddled. A lot of it can be fixed in editing. [02:31]
4. The editor writes in visual form and is the last barrier before release: "the story clears with us." [03:02]
5. Narrative structure = sequence of events, pacing, and the overall framework holding the story together. [04:00]
6. Visual structure = how elements are placed and balanced to guide the eye and create meaning and emotion. [04:00]
7. Frame the speaker off-centre, with depth behind and light on the face (her own setup as the example). [04:30]
8. Give even a YouTube video a clear introduction, an ending, and breaks in the middle. [04:30]
9. Keep viewers focused through faster cutting and pacing. Your own sense of "well paced" may bore a fresh viewer. [05:31]
10. Study what popular creators do to hold attention and adapt it to your niche. [06:05–06:35]
11. Always edit for the ordinary viewer ("regular old Joe"), not for film professionals. [07:02]
12. Nobody cares how long a shot took or how fancy it is. [07:02]
13. Throw out shots that are beautiful but pointless. [07:30]
14. Poor audio ruins a video faster than poor visuals. Clean audio and engaging music hold attention better than many visuals. [08:00]
15. Added sound effects held her own interest more than a few extra b-roll shots did. [08:00]
16. Show the cut to a trusted person for constructive criticism. Fresh eyes catch the slow parts, odd shots and stray sounds you stopped noticing. [08:34–09:35]

### 6.3 South Park's Writing Rule to Fix Any Story ("Therefore / But")
- **Source:** 6.3 | Patrick Walsh, Writing, presenting a clip of Trey Parker and Matt Stone at NYU | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ddnnIT0FuCc | 189 s (3:09) | 638 words | WEAK MATCH 0.45, but the Parker/Stone clip is quoted directly, so it is fully on topic.
- **Thesis:** Between every pair of beats in an outline, the connecting word should be "therefore" or "but", never "and then". Causation and complication build tension and pull the viewer forward.

**Rules**
1. Treat the outline as a list of beats and look at the word that joins each pair. [00:31]
2. If the word between beats is "and then", the story is boring and nobody will sit through it. [00:31]
3. Between **every** beat, the link should be **"therefore"** (causation) or **"but"** (complication). [01:01]
4. Alternate the two: this happens, therefore this, but this, therefore this. [01:01]
5. Literally write the beats out with the connector words between them to check. [01:01]
6. Each beat should affect the next ("that affects this, and that does that to that"). [01:01–01:30]
7. Causal and contrast links build tension, keep the viewer engaged, and make them want what comes next. [01:30]
8. A film built of "and then" beats leaves the viewer asking why they are watching. [02:01]
9. The presenter calls this rule the 80/20 of writing: if you follow only one rule, follow this one. [02:30]
(Context fact: South Park episodes were made in about **6 days** against an industry average of about **3 months**. [01:30–02:01])

### 6.4 How (Not) To Tell a Meaningful Zombie Story (filed as "How to Structure a Video Essay")
- **Source:** 6.4 | Like Stories of Old | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tU9nOycWiKo | 2022 s (33:42) | 5,098 words | search match 0.73. Not a video-essay structure tutorial. It is a thematic essay; structural lessons are extracted below. Sponsor reads at [00:00] and [31:39]–[32:32] have no technique.
- **Thesis:** Zombie stories last because they are a great *mechanic* (a survival/purpose fantasy with a simple repeatable cycle), not a great metaphor. They become meaningful only when the device is used deliberately to say something about people, not for novelty.

**Structural rules extracted from the argument**
1. Novelty for its own sake weakens meaning. A new variation (fast zombies, smart zombies) does not make a better story. [01:35, 31:00]
2. Before you build, ask what the story is really about, and when it works or fails. [02:06]
3. A setting makes an abstract point concrete (Dawn of the Dead's mall makes the consumerism critique legible). [04:06]
4. A protagonist needs an inner life: fears, desires, wants. A figure with none can only be an obstacle, not the lead. [05:02–06:01]
5. The only way to make such a figure a protagonist is to give it inner humanity (Warm Bodies). [06:01]
6. A strong, simple **mechanic** can carry a story and a genre further than a strong metaphor can. [07:02]
7. Reduce the world to one clear task. A "purpose fantasy" is a world of clear, simple meanings. [07:30–08:07]
8. Transform the setting so everything is either useful for the goal or discarded. [08:31–09:02]
9. The opposing force keeps the situation from resolving too early; it keeps the conflict going. [09:34–10:04]
10. Give the story a simple repeatable cycle: seek shelter, engage enemy, repeat (citing Entertain the Elk). [10:31]
11. Contrast heightens release: danger outside makes comfort in the safe zone feel stronger (Minecraft night/day, Dying Light safe zones). [10:31–11:02]
12. Make the threat beatable. Zombies are dangerous but outsmartable, which turns survival into a power fantasy. [11:31–12:35]
13. Make the protagonist accessible: an ordinary person, easy for the viewer to imagine being. [12:35–13:03]
14. Simple, followable rules ("follow a few basic rules") make viewers feel competent, and that feedback loop increases enjoyment. [13:03]
15. When a formula repeats and goes stale, writers escalate by switching the conflict type (zombies → conflict between survivors). [18:02]
16. Switching the conflict type changes the tone. Here it made the stories more cynical and depressing. Check what an escalation does to the overall message. [18:34–19:11]
17. A device's built-in assumptions carry meaning whether you intend it or not (infection assumes fear of other people and discourages caretaking). [19:11–20:01]
18. Anything can become meaningful if it is used consciously and thoughtfully. [20:34]
19. A clear arc gives a story its point: the selfish, negligent father learns to care (Train to Busan); the drifting man learns responsibility (Shaun of the Dead). [24:03–24:31]
20. Let the opposing force mirror the protagonist's own failings. [24:31]
21. An opposing force that is the exact opposite of the hero reveals the hero's limits and strengths (World War Z). [27:01–27:34, 29:05]
22. **Reveal the outcome at the start when you want assurance:** World War Z is told after the war is won, which gives "a light at the end of the tunnel" and frees the story to explore failures. [28:09–28:30]
23. End on the human quality the opposing force lacks (hope), and let the resolution show it. [29:05–29:31]
24. Close with a conclusion that restates the thesis: novelty is not needed, meaningful use is. [30:10–31:00]

**The essay's own structure (observed, usable as a template):** personal tension, liking something against one's better judgement [00:00–00:30] → the problem stated (meaning, not quality) [01:02–01:35] → the central question asked aloud [02:06] → an explicit promise ("Today, let's find out") [02:31] → origin/background [02:31–04:31] → the turn (it's a mechanic, not a metaphor) [07:02] → complication ("this is also where things start to get messy") [13:33] → the failure mode [18:02–21:33] → what works, with examples [22:01–29:31] → conclusion answering the opening question [30:10–31:00]. The question at 2:06 is answered at 30:10: a setup/payoff spanning the whole video.

### 6.5 Kurt Vonnegut on the Shapes of Stories
- **Source:** 6.5 | David Comberg (upload of the Vonnegut lecture) | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oP3c1h8v2ZQ | 276 s (4:36) | 608 words | WEAK MATCH 0.52, but it is the real lecture, so on topic.
- **Thesis:** A story can be drawn as a curve of the protagonist's fortune over time. The shape matters, not where it starts, and a few shapes (man in hole, boy gets girl, Cinderella) are endlessly popular.

**Rules**
1. Plot stories on two axes: vertical G–I (good fortune ↔ ill fortune: sickness and poverty down, wealth and good health up) and horizontal B–E (beginning ↔ end). [00:30]
2. The **shape** of the curve matters, not where it starts. [01:03]
3. **Man in hole:** start a little above average, fall into trouble, climb out, end higher. "People love that story." [01:03–01:33]
4. The labels are only memory aids: it needn't be a man or a hole. [01:03]
5. **Boy gets girl:** an average day, an average person expecting nothing, finds something wonderful, loses it, gets it back. [01:33–02:09]
6. Stories that stay below average (depressing days, depressing people) are less liked. [02:32]
7. **Cinderella**, called the most popular story in Western civilisation: start very low, at the maximum blow from fate (her mother's death). [02:09–03:03]
8. Then a **staircase of gifts**, each step up (shoes, stockings, transport, the dance). [03:30]
9. The drop (midnight) has a slight slope: the clock takes 20–30 s to strike 12. [04:04]
10. After the drop she does **not** fall back to the start, because she keeps the memory of the dance. A fall after a rise lands higher than the opening. [04:04]
11. End off the scale: the final rise goes beyond any earlier peak. [04:04–04:31]

### 6.6 The Right Way to Edit a Short Film (filed as "How to Edit a Story Out of Hours of Footage")
- **Source:** 6.6 | This Guy Edits (Sven Pape, ACE) | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZzOnMKGzWss | 823 s (13:43) | 2,146 words | search match 0.72. It is a scene review of a Nigerian short film, not a footage-triage tutorial. It gives story-turn and cut-motivation rules, but nothing on cutting down hours of footage.
- **Thesis:** The editor is the audience's ambassador. Cut only when something changes, wrap exposition inside emotion, mark story turns with a visual and musical shift, hit music on the reaction rather than the cut, and leave alone what already works.

**Rules**
1. The director guards the story; the editor is the "ambassador of the audience". [00:30]
2. Watch the film through once as a viewer before judging the edit. Stop only where a change is needed. [00:30, 02:30]
3. Establish status with a prop insert instead of dialogue (tech in the suitcase shows she is doing well financially). [01:07]
4. Check coverage before judging. What looked like plenty of coverage was mostly long takes. [03:02]
5. Move from wide/two-shot to singles as the scene becomes more intimate. [04:01, 05:30–06:02]
6. Make **each cut signify an emotional shift**. Let shots breathe and linger when nothing shifts (the director's intent, endorsed). [04:31]
7. A cut means "look over here". Cutting to something new tells the audience it matters, so do not cut without a reason. [05:00]
8. Deliver exposition so the viewer doesn't notice it is exposition: fold the plot information into banter and emotion. [05:00–05:30, 12:30]
9. Map the coverage pattern of a scene (insert → title → medium → entrance → time-jump insert → two-shot → mediums back and forth) to see its structure. [05:30–06:02]
10. Name the **story turn**: the moment "something new happens". [06:31]
11. A strong turn is a **withheld truth revealed**: the brother says what he had always decided not to tell her. [08:00]
12. Mark the turn visually. The turn happens against a blank wall, in contrast to the warm home before, which puts all the focus on him. [08:31]
13. Hit the music **on the listener's processing**, not on the cut: it lands as her eyes take it in and she nods. [09:02–09:32]
14. Music on the cut would be "on the nose" and less powerful. [09:32]
15. Each change in a character's emotional state (curious → understanding → sorry) is a further story turn. [10:00]
16. Use a character's physical action to show the shifting relationship (she pushes him away playfully, then comes over). [08:00, 11:03–11:32]
17. Normally cut into a shot with the person already partly in frame. Holding an empty frame while a character crosses it is fine when the walk is part of the story. [11:32]
18. Pay off an early setup at the end to show change (he now gets the bigger piece of chocolate, against the toy-car teasing earlier). [02:30, 13:01]
19. A story should show growth: the relationship at the end differs from the start. [13:01]
20. "Never change a winning team": if the cut works, give no notes. Don't edit just to edit. [12:30]

### 6.7 Every Great Story's Secret Weapon: Catharsis Explained (filed as "Information Rationing")
- **Source:** 6.7 | StudioBinder | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=owxQGzgipaY | 981 s (16:21) | 1,904 words | search match 0.72. **Not the information-rationing video.** Its subject is catharsis: setup, payoff, stakes and ending. Ad read at [15:31]–[16:01].
- **Thesis:** Catharsis is the exhale after the inhale. Setup takes most of the runtime and builds emotion; the payoff releases it by resolving the primary tension, made stronger by stakes, an arc, and visible reactions.

**Rules**
1. Structure the story as inhale (build-up to the climax) and exhale (catharsis/release). [00:00]
2. Catharsis = purging or release of built-up emotion (Aristotle, *Poetics*). In modern narrative it means the emotional release at the end of any story, not only tragedy. [01:00, 02:00–02:32]
3. Catharsis is subjective. The same ending moves one viewer and leaves another cold. [01:32–02:00]
4. Aristotle's 2×2: knowingly vs unknowingly × completed vs stopped at the last moment. [02:32–03:00]
5. Weakest: **knowingly and stopped**. The audience reads it as an idle threat (The Searchers). [03:00–04:03]
6. **Knowingly and completed**: the character knows it is wrong and does it anyway (Se7en). [04:03–05:06]
7. Second best: **unknowingly and completed**. The truth arrives too late, which maximises pity (Oldboy). [05:06–05:33]
8. Highest potential: **unknowingly and stopped**, because the character can reflect on what they nearly did (Batman v Superman, "Martha"). [06:23–07:02]
9. A happy ending becomes cathartic by **resolving the primary tension** of the story (When Harry Met Sally: friends → together). [07:31–08:33]
10. Find the tension that really drives the story and root the catharsis there (Shawshank: the release is the reunion with Red, not the escape). [09:01]
11. Catharsis can turn a tragic ending triumphant (Gladiator). [09:34]
12. A missing catharsis can make a "victory" read as tragic (Blade Runner). [09:34–10:02]
13. The ending recolours how the audience reads everything before it. [10:34]
14. Catharsis has two parts: **setup**, which is "the vast majority of a narrative", and **payoff**. [10:34–11:00]
15. Withhold the thing the audience wants for most of the runtime (Sleepless in Seattle's leads barely meet until the end). The longer it is held, the bigger the release. [11:00]
16. The payoff must build on the setup and give closure, through either overcoming the obstacle or tragedy. [11:00–11:33]
17. A payoff can carry triumph and tragedy at once (Titanic). [11:33–12:00]
18. **Stakes:** the audience must know why the triumph or tragedy matters. [12:00–12:32]
19. Triumph needs great effort against the odds (the underdog). [12:32]
20. A character arc strengthens catharsis: positive for triumph (Rocky), negative for tragedy (Macbeth). [12:32–13:35]
21. Show how characters and onlookers react to the cathartic event; their release prompts ours (Miracle, Good Will Hunting). [13:35–14:00]
22. A reaction can also complicate the catharsis (Schindler: "I didn't do enough"). [14:00–15:03]

### 6.8 The New Rules of YouTube (2027) (filed as "How MrBeast Structures Videos")
- **Source:** 6.8 | Colin and Samir with Paddy Galloway | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WyiGFFTnIS4 | 3925 s (65:25) | 13,522 words | search match 0.61. A strategy conversation, not a MrBeast structure breakdown. The transcript is a machine back-translation: names are garbled ("Michel Carré" = Michelle Khare, "ManMade", "Listers"), but meaning is recoverable. Read in full. Much of it is long-form/TV strategy; structure and retention points are extracted, and macro strategy is kept short and tagged.
- **Thesis:** As YouTube moves to TV and content becomes abundant, wins come from substance that keeps a clicked viewer satisfied: a format worth repeating, a human with something only they can say, a fast first 15 s, then room to breathe.

**Structure and retention rules**
1. TV is now the largest device for big channels: from 23–24% (2021) to over 50% of client audience; creators report 40–50%. [01:33–02:00]
2. Average long-form length is up **51% since 2020** (12–16 min typical then, 20+ min now). [02:34]
3. On a big screen, a 31-min video with fewer cuts, no fast editing and no narration can win (Red Bull Everest ski, 30M+ views). CONTEXT: TV long-form. [04:30–05:01]
4. Set length by what the best material deserves ("is the best footage 30 minutes long?"), not by habit. Cutting great material short is a loss. [06:00–06:31]
5. Switching cost on a TV remote is high, so viewers look for a familiar, show-like experience. [07:04]
6. Between a great 12-min and a great 24-min version, the strategist picks the 24-min. Shorter is still better for some content. [08:30]
7. Homepage space for long-form is shrinking (Shorts blocks). What matters is whether a viewer stays once they click. [10:00–12:01]
8. Average view duration for long-form rose **77%** (4:35 → 8:00) from 2021 to 2025. [13:00]
9. Optimise for repeat viewers and a relationship, not just view maximising. [16:02]
10. One failed video is not punished forever (striker analogy). Experiment. [16:30–17:31]
11. Make at least **1 in every 4–5 videos** the one you want to make, not one optimised for title and thumbnail. [17:31–18:00]
12. Honesty and vulnerability build the relationship; a "had to make it" video connected deeply (120,000 views in a month). [18:00–19:31]
13. With ~20 million uploads a day, any strategically obvious video is already being made. Ask "what do I actually want to say?" and find the perspective only you have. [18:30–19:00, 58:01]
14. Measure **satisfaction**, not just views. "Only Red Bull could do this" comments are the signal. [21:31–22:00]
15. A great title and thumbnail with no substance will stop working. [25:00]
16. View count is a weakened signal: a dubbed version can get **4×** the views of the same video undubbed. [25:31]
17. Optimise for the audience you sell to (a US product needs US views, not overseas views). [26:00]
18. When a format works, "keep digging": re-make it across new situations before others copy it. [26:30–27:30]
19. Abstract the format; don't take it literally. The format is not "a new puppy" but "my dog meets a weaker, shy dog". [35:00]
20. A channel *is* a format. Let it evolve gradually into a show with consistent branding, not a big launch. [35:30–36:01]
21. A show frame (intro, title card, logo) changes how viewers read the creator's purpose ("Finding Meaning"). Only the intro, style and title graphics need to change. [33:01–33:31]
22. Model it on Top Gear, not Breaking Bad: episodic, a set format, no deep serial story. [34:02]
23. **The story is the transformation, not the event.** Not "the dogs meet" but a fearful dog breaking out of its shell. [36:30–37:01]
24. Share the human backstory. A top creator's audience didn't know he quit school at 15 to start the channel; "people are looking at human beings". [30:30–31:00]
25. Once spectacle is democratised, individuality comes to the front. [31:36]
26. The "everything" / comprehensive format works: it does effort the viewer won't do, and signals a complete picture (a huge curiosity gap). [44:30–46:31]
27. Don't take "everything" literally either: "I watched a lot, here are the best 4" still works. [45:00–45:30]
28. A quest structure: the first 2–3 minutes are spent figuring out where the place is. [47:32]
29. **End the video when the goal is reached** (arrive at the location, finish the build). Completion is the ending. [48:00]
30. A thumbnail needs context: open space showing it sits under a staircase made the difference (said as 17 vs 3, units garbled). [49:31–50:01]

### B.2 Short-form ad translation: script → selects → beat map

#### B.2.1 The beat model (6 beats onto 3 acts and the plot points)

| Beat | Story function (source) | Job in a talking-head ad | Window (% of runtime) | At 30 s | At 45 s | At 60 s |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **H Hook** | Cold open / teaser + opening (6.1 [02:30–03:07]); confirm the click + add a stake (6.8 [52:04]) | Name the topic and who it's for; optionally tease the proof number | 0–10% (DERIVED; topic ≤ 2 s from Module 4 [4.1]) | 0–3 s | 0–4.5 s | 0–6 s |
| **P Problem** | Inciting incident = want + obstacle (6.1 [04:00]) | The customer's want (calls, customers) and what blocks it | 8–25% | 2.5–7.5 s | 3.5–11 s | 5–15 s |
| **A Agitation** | Rising action / Act 2 tension (6.1 [05:30]); "but" reframe; stakes (6.7 [12:00]) | Why it's happening (the mechanism) and what it costs, every day | 20–45% | 6–13.5 s | 9–20 s | 12–27 s |
| **PR Proof** | Midpoint turn (6.1 [06:02]); "something new happens" (6.6 [06:31]); man-in-hole low point → climb (6.5) | A real client, a real number: the reversal | starts ≤ 50%, number lands ≤ 55% (DERIVED from the 6.1 midpoint) | by 15 s / 16.5 s | by 22.5 s / 25 s | by 30 s / 33 s |
| **O Offer** | Act 3 / resolution of the want–obstacle (6.1 [10:34]); payoff of each setup (6.7 [11:00]) | What you get, each item answering a problem item; the price; risk removed | 50–88% | 15–26.5 s | 22.5–39.5 s | 30–53 s |
| **C CTA** | Resolution + "end when the goal is reached" (6.8 [48:00]); answer the opening question (6.1 [11:05]) | Ask once, name the brand last, stop | last 8–15% (≥ 85% start, HOUSE) | 25.5–30 s | 38–45 s | 51–60 s |

Act mapping (DERIVED from 6.1 [05:30]): **Act 1 = H+P ≈ 15–30%**, **Act 2 = A+PR (+ the start of O) ≈ 40–60%**, **Act 3 = O+C ≈ 25–40%**. Act 3 in an ad is longer than in film because the offer has to be spelled out; Act 2 should still be the largest single act or close to it.

Turning points as % of runtime (DERIVED, tune on data):
- **TP1, inciting incident / first act break** (the viewer feels the problem is theirs): 15–25%.
- **TP2, midpoint reversal** (proof: "but this client went from X to Y"): 40–55%.
- **TP3, second act break** (the decision point: price plus risk removed, "no big budgets"): 60–75%.
- **Resolution** (CTA plus brand): last 10%, and the video ends within 0.5 s of the last brand word (HOUSE).

Intro pacing (from 6.9 [01:01–02:30], scaled DERIVED): the long-form formula "open fast, establish scale, go to the first challenge" maps onto H→P. The "first challenge" is the problem beat, which already starts at 8–25%. Nothing that is background (who we are, how long we have been doing this) sits before P. The 1.1 s visual-change rate of 6.9's intro is met in the hook by a combination of cut/punch-in changes, caption page changes and cutaway build steps. It is not met by shorter A-roll shots alone (see B.7 #10). Tension renewal every few minutes (6.9 [02:30]) becomes a renewal at every beat boundary, which is what the therefore/but chain in B.2.2 produces.

#### B.2.2 Connector rules for an ad (from 6.3)
- **Between beats:** every boundary H→P, P→A, A→PR, PR→O, O→C must read as *therefore* or *but*. Typical ad shapes:
  - H→P: "**If** you're not there → your calls go to competitors" (causal).
  - P→A: "That's **not because** they're better, **but** because their listing is" (contrast reframe).
  - A→PR: "**But** when we fixed exactly that for X…" (contrast: the reversal).
  - PR→O: "**So** now it's your turn" (causal). "**And** now it's your turn" is an additive and fails.
  - O→C: "**So** if you want top 3, you know whom to call" (causal).
- **Inside a beat,** an additive list is allowed only when it is a list of one kind (symptoms, features) and each item builds with the words on screen (EDIT-STANDARD §1). A list of 3–4 is fine; a list that runs past one beat's window is "and then" and fails.
- The connector can be **implicit** when the edit does the linking (a cutaway that visibly contradicts the previous line), but the gate below then needs a human to confirm it.

#### B.2.3 Information rationing for an ad (DERIVED from 6.7 [11:00], 6.6 [08:00], 6.1 [02:30–03:07], 6.4 [28:09], 6.8 [52:04])
Give away early: **the topic, who it's for, and (optionally) the outcome**. The teaser/assurance pattern (6.1 cold open, 6.4 World War Z) makes the claim credible from second 1. Hold back, in this order:
1. **The mechanism** (why competitors win) until after the problem lands. It is the "but" that reframes. (6.6 withheld truth as a turn.)
2. **The proof specifics** (client name + number) until after agitation. This is the midpoint reversal. A teaser may state the number in the hook, but the *who and how* stays back.
3. **The price** until after the proof. Price before proof is a payoff with no setup.
4. **The brand name** as the final word. It is the answer to "whom do I contact?" (6.1 [11:05]: answer the question the film raised). A logo bug can sit on screen earlier; the spoken brand comes last.
5. **Each new beat adds at least one fact not given before.** No beat restates an earlier one, except a deliberate callback that pays off a setup (6.6 [13:01]), which a human reviewer flags as such.

#### B.2.4 Script-to-selects procedure (executable)
Input: the raw whisper word-timing JSON (same `[start, end, word]` triples as `Edit/v2_words.json`). Output: `beats.json` (beat map) feeding the `beat` field of `edl_v2.json`.
1. **Sentence-segment** the raw words on punctuation and gaps > 0.6 s. Keep start/end times per sentence.
2. **Take-group:** cluster sentences that are re-takes of the same line (≥ 60% shared content words, or the same opening 3 words). Within a group pick the **last complete take that passes `gate_stutter.py`**. If two pass, pick the one with fewer fillers and tighter timing. (Fresh eyes and fewer flaws: 6.2 [08:34]; useful or discarded: 6.4 [08:31].)
3. **Label each surviving sentence** with one role H/P/A/PR/O/C or X (cut). Use Claude over the text, with the definitions in B.2.1. X = anything that fails "useful or discarded" (6.4 [08:31]) or is beautiful but pointless (6.2 [07:30]).
4. **Work backwards** (6.1 [12:00]): lock C first (the complete sign-off, matching the existing Ending gate). Then O: list the features. Then P/A: keep the problem items that the features pay off. Then PR. Then choose H last: the line that best names the topic in ≤ 2 s, or a teaser of PR's number.
5. **Setup→payoff map:** make a two-column table, problem items ↔ offer items. Drop or rewrite any problem item with no payoff, and any offer item with no setup, unless it is a clearly framed bonus ("We **also** give you…").
6. **Order** the selects H→P→A→PR→O→C. Re-ordering is allowed (the teaser is a line moved up from PR).
7. **Write the connector chain** out literally (6.3 [01:01]): `H [therefore] P [but] A [but] PR [therefore] O [therefore] C`. Wherever the chain reads "and", either pick a different take that starts with a causal or contrast word, or move a sentence so the contrast sits at the boundary. Never synthesise words (EDIT-STANDARD §3: no generative voice).
8. **Time it:** place the selects with the house pause rules (≤ 0.25 s gaps), compute each beat's % of runtime, and run the gates in B.5. If a beat is outside its window, trim inside the over-long beat first (Act 2 overruns are usually agitation lists).
9. **Mark the turn** in the EDL: the PR start gets the strongest visual change in the cut (full-screen cutaway, or the one extreme punch-in, per EDIT-STANDARD §1), and the music lift sits on the number's reaction moment, not the cut frame (6.6 [09:02]).

#### B.2.6 What does NOT transfer
- **Long breathing takes, no narration, 31-min TV pacing** (6.8 [05:01]) and **"shots breathe and linger"** (6.6 [04:31]): the house grammar (A-roll 2–4 s, cutaways 3–8 s, 40–55% cutaway) and the 0.25 s pause gate win in a 30–60 s Reel. What transfers is the *principle*: cut on a shift, not on a timer.
- **Act 2 at 50% with a dark night of the soul and a do-or-die decision** (6.1 [08:04]): an ad has no protagonist journey on screen. The "decision" becomes the viewer's, at the price + risk-removal line.
- **Character reaction shots at the catharsis** (6.7 [13:35]): usually no client footage. Substitute a reaction proxy: a count-up of the number, or a client review card. Do not stage a fake reaction (6.8 [57:00]: access and real people are the edge).
- **Aristotle's knowingly/unknowingly quadrants** (6.7 [02:32–07:02]): tragedy mechanics, no direct use in a service ad.
- **Show/format strategy, TV share, AVD growth, dubbing, curation** (6.8): channel strategy. The usable translation is the *campaign*: a repeatable ad format (same beat map, new client proof each time), 6.8 [26:30, 35:00].
- **Zombie-genre meaning, cynicism of tone** (6.4 [18:34–21:33]): no direct transfer. Kept only as "escalation changes tone": fear-led agitation that runs too long turns a helpful ad into a threat.
- **1080p cap to avoid looking over-produced** (6.9 [04:02]): EDIT-STANDARD §2 requires the highest quality. What transfers is the native *feel* (selfie framing, natural delivery, an organic moment kept), not lower resolution.
- **Thumbnail craft, merch sales, back-catalogue licensing, mission-driven business** (6.9 [04:32–09:00]): channel business. Only the cover-frame rules (B.1 #35) and the CTA formula (B.1 #36, with real scarcity only) carry over.
- **17 s to the first challenge** (6.9 [02:00]): an absolute number for a 15-min video. In a 30–60 s ad the equivalent is the P beat at 8–25% of runtime.

### B.3 Checklist (binary)

**Pre-edit**
- [ ] Raw transcribed with word timings; sentences segmented; re-takes grouped.
- [ ] Each kept sentence labelled H/P/A/PR/O/C/X.
- [ ] Ending (C) chosen first and the full sign-off exists in the raw.
- [ ] Setup→payoff table written: every offer item has a problem item, and every problem item has an offer item (or is cut).
- [ ] A real, specific proof exists (client name + number). If not, flag to the client before cutting.
- [ ] Connector chain written out literally (`H [therefore] P [but] A …`) with no "and" at any boundary.

**During edit**
- [ ] The topic word lands in ≤ 2 s.
- [ ] The hook confirms what the ad is about, then adds a stake or teaser.
- [ ] No background or credentials line sits before the problem beat. (6.9 [02:00])
- [ ] In the hook, something visibly changes at least every ~1.5 s: a cut or punch-in, a caption page or a cutaway build step. (6.9 [01:01])
- [ ] Each beat boundary renews tension (a new problem, a reframe, a reversal, a number); the middle has no flat stretch. (6.9 [02:30])
- [ ] Each beat adds at least one new fact; no beat restates another (callbacks marked).
- [ ] The proof starts by 50% and its number lands by 55%.
- [ ] Price comes after the proof number.
- [ ] The proof boundary carries the strongest visual change in the cut.
- [ ] The music lift lands on the number/reaction, not the cut frame.
- [ ] No beautiful-but-pointless cutaway (each cutaway illustrates the line under it).
- [ ] Problem/agitation stays below 45% of runtime (the hole is not too deep or too long).

**Pre-delivery**
- [ ] Beat order H→P→A→PR→O→C holds (teaser excepted).
- [ ] The CTA starts at or after 85%; the last spoken word is the brand; the picture ends within 0.5 s of it.
- [ ] The opening question/problem is explicitly answered by the end.
- [ ] The ad ends on its high point: the last beat is the most positive in tone (benefit, confidence), not the fear. (6.9 [04:02], 6.5)
- [ ] Any scarcity in the CTA (limited slots, a deadline, a bonus) is true for this client's offer. (6.9 [07:30])
- [ ] Watched once by fresh eyes (someone who has not seen the cut), who can say the problem, the proof and the ask.
- [ ] The Words gate script = the **re-ordered selects script**, not the raw order.

### B.4 QA (human-judgement review)

| Question the senior editor asks | What good looks like |
|---|---|
| Read only the beat-boundary sentences aloud. Do they form a chain of "so… but… so…"? | Each boundary sentence answers or complicates the previous one; no "and also". [6.3] |
| After the hook, do I know what this is about, and do I have a reason to stay? | Topic plus a stake or teaser ("…and one restaurant got 30+ calls a day doing this"). [6.8 52:04, 6.1 03:07] |
| Is the problem a want I have, blocked by something specific? | "Calls go to competitors" (want = calls; obstacle = their listing beats mine). [6.1 04:00] |
| Is there a moment where something new happens? | The reframe ("not because they're better") or the proof reversal clearly changes direction. [6.6 06:31] |
| Is the proof real and specific? | Named client, concrete number, ideally on-screen evidence (review card, call log). [6.8 57:00, 6.7 12:00] |
| Does every promise in the offer answer something I was told was wrong? | Each feature visibly answers a problem item; no orphan features or orphan problems. [6.7 11:00] |
| Does the fortune curve go down then up, ending higher than the start? | Mild tension in P/A, clear lift at PR, confident close; the ad does not end on fear. [6.5] |

### B.5 QC (automatable gates)

All gates run on the **final mix re-transcribed with whisper.cpp word timings** (as `gate_words.py` already does), plus
`beats.json` (sentence → role, from Claude labelling) and `edl_v2.json`. `T` = runtime of the final.

| ID | Gate | Metric & how to measure | Pass | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| N1 | Beat completeness & order | Sequence of role labels per sentence from `beats.json`; collapse runs; compare with H,P,A,PR,O,C | All 6 present, in order (an H-position teaser copied from PR allowed once) | 6.1 [01:34], 6.2 [02:01] |
| N2 | Topic latency | Start time of the first topic keyword (per-ad list: "Google", "Maps", "listing", business category) in the word timings | ≤ 2.0 s | Module 4 R1 / 4.1 [02:00]; 6.1 [03:07] "first few seconds" |
| N3 | Boundary connectors | For each beat boundary, look for a causal word (so, therefore, because, that's why, which means, if, so that, now, that's how) or a contrast word (but, however, yet, instead, not because, actually, until, except, still) in the last 4 words of beat k or the first 4 words of beat k+1. Additive-only (and, also, and then, plus, next) = fail. No word found = "implicit" → Claude classifies the pair as cause/contrast/additive, then a human confirms | 100% of boundaries cause/contrast; 0 additive; implicit ≤ 1 and human-confirmed | 6.3 [01:01] "between every beat" |
| N4 | Within-beat list length | Count of consecutive additive-joined items inside one beat | ≤ 4 items, and the list stays inside its beat window | DERIVED from 6.3 + EDIT-STANDARD §1 (lists build line by line) |
| N5 | No restatement | For each pair of beats, Jaccard overlap of content lemmas (stop-words removed) plus a Claude "same claim?" check | Jaccard < 0.5 and no "same claim" unless tagged `callback` in `beats.json` | DERIVED from 6.3 (each beat must move the story), 6.7 (payoff ≠ repeat) |
| N6 | New information per beat | Count new named entities, numbers or offer nouns first seen in each beat | ≥ 1 per beat | DERIVED (rationing) |
| N7 | Proof position | `t_PR_start / T` and `t_number_end / T`, where `t_number_end` = end time of the proof's number token ("30", "thirty") | PR start ≤ 0.50; number ≤ 0.55 (HOUSE; warn 0.55–0.60) | DERIVED from 6.1 [06:02] midpoint ≈ half |
| N8 | Price after proof | First price token time (₹, digits next to "month", "rupees", "only") vs `t_number_end` | price > proof | DERIVED (rationing order) |
| N9 | Act proportions | Act1 = H+P, Act2 = A+PR, Act3 = O+C durations / T | Act1 15–30%, Act2 30–50%, Act3 25–45% (HOUSE) | DERIVED from 6.1 [05:30] |
| N10 | CTA window & clean ending | CTA start / T; last word = brand token; picture end − brand word end | CTA start ≥ 0.85 (HOUSE); last word is the brand; tail ≤ 0.5 s | 6.8 [48:00], 6.1 [11:05]; EDIT-STANDARD Ending gate |
| N11 | Setup→payoff coverage | Claude maps each P/A item to an O item and vice versa; count orphans | 0 orphan problems; 0 orphan offers unless framed with "also"/bonus | 6.7 [11:00], 6.6 [13:01] |
| N12 | Fortune curve | Claude scores each sentence's valence −2…+2 for the viewer's position; check the minimum and the end | Minimum falls in P/A; mean valence of C > mean of H; P+A ≤ 45% of T; the final sentence scores ≥ +1 (end on a high point) | 6.5 [01:03–04:31] (man in hole); 6.4 [18:34] (don't over-darken); 6.9 [04:02] (high-point ending) |
| N13 | Turn emphasis | In the EDL, the transition type at PR start; cross-check with an ffmpeg `scene` score / frame-diff spike at that time | PR start has a full-screen cutaway, whip or the extreme punch-in; its frame-diff is among the top 3 of the cut | 6.6 [08:31] turn marked visually |

| N15 | Cut motivation | Every cut in the EDL sits at a sentence boundary or a beat-labelled shift (not mid-word); cutaway text matches the spoken line | 100% (mid-word cuts already fail the Words gate) | 6.6 [05:00]; EDIT-STANDARD §1 "nothing appears before it's said" |
| N16 | Take selection | Every kept sentence is the last complete take of its group and passes `gate_stutter.py` | 100% (an earlier take is allowed only with a logged reason) | 6.2 [08:34]; EDIT-STANDARD Stutters gate |
| N17 | Hook visual-change rate | Merge the times of visual events in the first 20% of T (or the first 6 s, whichever is shorter): EDL cuts and punch-in/zoom changes from `edl_v2.json`, caption page changes from the caption track, cutaway build-step starts from the motion log; cross-check with ffmpeg `select='gt(scene,0.1)'` / frame-diff spikes. ### B.6 Anti-patterns / failure modes

| Anti-pattern | Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "And then" chain | Boundary words are "and", "also", "plus"; feature list sprawls across beats | Choose takes that start with "so"/"but", or move the contrast sentence to the boundary. [6.3] |
| Brochure order | Features before problem; price before proof | Re-order to H→P→A→PR→O→C; price after the proof number. [6.1, 6.7] |
| Orphan setup | A problem item never answered ("what type of reviews to collect") | Cut it or add the matching offer line. [6.7 11:00] |
| Orphan payoff | A feature with no reason given | Add the problem line from the raw, or frame it explicitly as a bonus ("also"). [6.7] |
| Restated beat | Two beats say the same claim in different words | Keep the stronger take; cut the other unless it is a deliberate callback. [6.3] |
| Late proof | Proof number after 60% of runtime | Shorten the hook list or agitation; or tease the number in the hook. [6.1 06:02, 6.1 03:07] |
| Vague teaser | Hook is pure suspense with no topic | Name the topic in ≤ 2 s; the teaser adds a stake, it does not replace clarity. [6.8 52:04; Module 4] |
| Bottomless hole | Agitation > 45% of runtime, fear-heavy tone | Trim symptoms to the 3 that the offer pays off; reach the proof sooner. [6.5, 6.4 18:34] |
| Packaging without substance | Hook promises more than the proof delivers | Use only a claim the client footage or data supports. [6.8 25:00] |
| Generic claim | "We'll grow your business" with no specific client, number or access | Lead with the real client and real number; that is the edge. [6.8 57:00, 58:30] |
| Unmotivated cut / pointless cutaway | Cutaway graphics that don't illustrate the line under them | Every cut marks a shift; every cutaway builds with the words. [6.6 05:00, 6.2 07:30] |
| Music on the cut | The hit lands exactly on the cutaway frame | Shift the hit 0.2–1.0 s later onto the number landing. [6.6 09:32] |
| Tail after the goal | Extra lines or a second CTA after the brand | End on the brand word. [6.8 48:00] |
| Edit churn | A version that passed gets re-cut by taste | "Never change a winning team": only fix failed gates or notes. [6.6 12:30] |
| Self-blind pacing | The editor thinks it flows; a fresh viewer finds the middle slow | A fresh-eyes view before delivery. [6.2 05:31, 08:34] |
| Energy sag after the hook | Fast hook, then a long run with no new information or visual event; retention drops mid-ad | Renew tension at every beat boundary; cut the coasting list items (N19). [6.9 02:30] |
| Explanation before story | Credentials or "who we are" before the problem | Move it after the problem or cut it; start the storyline first (N18). [6.9 02:00] |
| Cuts as a substitute for structure | Many cuts and fast talk, but a muddled order; the viewer still drifts | Fix the beat order and connectors first; cuts support structure. [6.9 00:30] |
| Over-produced feel | Glossy template look, every organic moment scrubbed out; it reads as an ad, not a person | Keep the selfie feel and one natural moment; keep technical quality. [6.9 03:30–04:02] |
| Flat ending | Last line is a dry instruction or the fear restated | End on the most positive line, then the brand. [6.9 04:02] |

### B.7 Conflicts & open questions

1. **Cold-open teaser vs EDIT-STANDARD §1 "Nothing appears before it's said."** 6.1 [02:30–03:07] recommends showing a later high-stakes moment first. **Call:** allowed only as a *spoken* line moved to the front (re-ordered audio). Graphics still follow the words, so the number card appears when the number is said in the teaser, and again at the proof.
2. **"Let shots breathe / each cut = emotional shift" (6.6 [04:31]) and "let the audience breathe" (6.8 [53:02]) vs the edit rhythm (A-roll 2–4 s, cutaways 3–8 s; style `grammar.*`) and the 0.25 s pause gate (§3).** **Call:** keep those numbers. In a Reel, "breathe" means a held cutaway (the count-up runs while the voice continues), not a speech gap. Open question for the reviewer: allow one deliberate ≤ 0.4 s held beat after the proof number, exempted from the Pauses gate by a tag in the EDL?
3. **No structure gate in EDIT-STANDARD §4.** All current gates are technical (words, pauses, loudness, picture). A cut can pass every gate and still be a "brochure order" ad. **Call:** add N1, N3, N7, N10 and N11 as structure gates; N12–N14 as warnings until calibrated on more ads.
4. **Words gate vs re-ordering.** §4 compares the cut "with the script". Once selects are re-ordered, "the script" must be the **re-ordered selects script** written out in B.2.4, or the gate will flag the deliberate re-order as missing or duplicated words.
5. **Generative voice ban (§3) limits connector fixes.** "And now it's your turn" cannot be edited into "So now…" without synthesising speech. **Call:** fix connectors only by take choice or re-ordering; if no causal take exists, let a cutaway carry the contrast and send the client a re-record note for next time.
6. **Ending gate alignment.** §4 "the sign-off is complete" matches 6.1 [11:05] and 6.8 [48:00]. No conflict; N10 adds "nothing after it".
7. **Act proportions.** 6.1's ≈25/50/25 is for documentaries. In the worked example the ad runs ≈22/35/43, because the offer needs room. **Call:** the HOUSE windows in N9 (Act 3 up to 45%) accept this; recalibrate after 5+ ads with Meta retention data.
8. **Percent windows are DERIVED, not sourced.** No transcript in this module gives ad-specific timing; only Module 4 [4.1] gives the 1–2 s topic number. Treat N7, N9, N10 and N14 thresholds as starting points and tune them against Meta's audience-retention curves (drop-offs at boundaries are the signal that a connector or turn failed).
9. **Missing source.** The curriculum's "Information Rationing" video (6.7) was replaced by a catharsis video, and the "MrBeast structure" video (6.8) by a strategy podcast. 6.9 has since been added and covers the MrBeast structure gap (intro formula, tension recurrence, high-point ending). Re-sourcing 6.7 would still strengthen B.2.3 and the timing windows. 6.6 also has nothing on reducing hours of footage; B.2.4's take-grouping is my procedure, not the source's.
10. **6.9 "visual change every 1.1 s" vs EDIT-STANDARD §1 house rhythm (A-roll 2–4 s).** Read literally, 6.9 would mean sub-1.5 s A-roll shots. **Call:** keep the 2–4 s A-roll shots. A "visual change" counts any cut, punch-in step, caption page change or cutaway build step; N17 measures all of them together in the hook only.
11. **6.9 "never look too produced" (1080p cap) vs EDIT-STANDARD §2 (highest quality, no added artefacts).** **Call:** keep the technical quality. Get the native feel from content (selfie framing, natural delivery, one organic moment), never from lower resolution or added degradation.
12. **6.9 relentless front-loading vs 6.8 "let the audience breathe" after 15 s.** These are consistent: both front-load the intro, then 6.8 eases the pace while 6.9 renews tension at intervals. For a 30–60 s ad, the whole runtime is close to an intro, so the house rhythm applies throughout and "breathe" stays as B.7 #2.
13. **6.9's CTA sales formula (exclusivity, extra incentive, time limit) vs truthfulness.** **Call:** use it only when the client's offer really has a limit or bonus. Invented scarcity is a substance-over-packaging failure (B.1 #19).
