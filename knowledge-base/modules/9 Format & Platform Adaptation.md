# Module 9: Format & Platform Adaptation

> **Note (style profiles).** This module was written while CVES was tested on its first ad. Its research rules are universal; where it speaks of the "house" look or a house number for a per-client choice (palette, accent colour, grade, transition kinds, cutaway share, caption position, bed level), read it as the value from the client's style profile (`docs/STYLE.md`). The black stage / green accent / warm moody A-roll measured here is now the optional preset `profitcast-dark`, not the default.

Target: talking-head selfie ads, 9:16, 1080x1920, 30–60 s, run as **paid Meta ads in Instagram/Facebook Reels**.
Edit standard: `knowledge-base/EDIT-STANDARD.md`.

**What the eight sources actually are.** Only 9.5, 9.6 and 9.7 are hands-on vertical-editing tutorials. 9.1 is creator strategy (Shorts vs long-form), 9.2 is a general YouTube editing tutorial, 9.3 is about how recommendation algorithms work (no infinite-loop video existed), 9.4 is a 3-minute Adobe Express sponsor spot with no pixel numbers, and 9.8 is a long-form YouTube podcast about TV viewing and intros. The brief's required numbers (safe-zone px, caption reading speed, hook length, export specs) are therefore mostly **not in the transcripts**. Where I supply them I name the outside norm (Meta Ads Guide, Netflix Timed Text Style Guide, BBC Subtitle Guidelines) and mark it **[NORM]**. Anything I worked out myself from the frame geometry is marked **[DERIVED]**. Every other rule traces to a transcript timestamp.

### 9.1 YouTube Shorts VS Longs (My new strategy for growth)
- **Source:** 9.1 · "YouTube Shorts VS Longs (My new strategy for growth)" · Think Media (Sean Cannell) · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-HSU74pHvgk · 1669 s · 5,331 words · WEAK MATCH (0.28). It is about channel strategy, not editing or pacing. It is partly relevant: it describes the vertical-feed viewer, how the format loops, CTA limits and native vs repurposed content. It contains no editing numbers.
- **Thesis:** Shorts trade depth for reach. They are quick to make, easy to finish, loop by default and are watched by a lower-attention, low-commitment audience. Long-form builds authority and money. Make each format natively for its own feed.

Rules:
1. Treat short vertical video as quick to produce: shoot on a phone and add text and music in-app. Don't over-produce the basic format. [00:32]
2. Expect short videos to get more views, and don't mistake views for impact. Measure the business outcome. [01:01]
3. Use shorts to build "content muscle" when time or skills are limited. [01:32], [17:30]
4. Design for completion. Finishing a sub-60 s video is far more likely than finishing a masterclass, and completion is the engagement signal for the format. [01:32–02:01]
5. Make the short a "sample" of the full offer, like a free taste at a food stall. Most samplers move on and a few buy. [02:01–02:30]
6. Make one vertical master and post it on every vertical platform (Instagram, X, Facebook, LinkedIn, TikTok). [02:30]
7. Expect vertical viewers to have shorter attention spans than long-form viewers, and edit for that. [04:32]
8. Don't count on the vertical feed for depth. It is "limited by the 60 seconds", so deliver one point, not nuance. [04:00–04:32]
9. Expect a short lifespan for shorts and an evergreen one for search-led content. [07:00]
10. Know that organic Shorts have almost no clickable CTA (YouTube removed description and pinned-comment links). In-feed CTAs are weak, and the feed's norm discourages hard CTAs. [07:00–08:00] (Paid Meta ads differ: the ad unit carries a CTA button. See B.2.)
11. Accept that short-form audiences rarely cross over to long-form. Treat each piece as standalone. [08:00]
12. You cannot change a Short's thumbnail later or upload a custom one, so frame 1 is your thumbnail. [08:30]
13. A short has no chapters and can't be scrubbed. It "loops" and holds the viewer hostage, so the whole piece must play straight through and loop back. [08:30–09:00]
14. Don't make vertical content radically different from the brand's main content. Keep the visual branding consistent so viewers recognise the brand across formats. [19:01–20:01]
15. A clip cut from a long podcast works as a short only when the moment is complete in itself. [23:32–24:01]
16. Prefer native shorts, designed for vertical from the start, over chopping a list video into one short per tip. [23:32–24:31]
17. Don't rely on volume AI-repurposing as a quality strategy. Craft the short "strategically to be shorts". [24:31]
18. The packaging recipe is topic, title, thumbnail and content, done well. A good piece has a long shelf life. [25:32]
19. Prefer fewer, higher-effort pieces over a lot of low-effort content. [26:00]
20. Decide the format from the goal (views, money, authority, leads) before you edit. Chasing views can be the wrong metric. [15:33–16:01]

### 9.2 How To Edit YouTube Videos in 2026 (Ultimate Guide)
- **Source:** 9.2 · "How To Edit YouTube Videos in 2026 (Ultimate Guide)" · finzar · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xmfSCKV7qUE · 1331 s · 4,002 words · search match 0.71. Partial match: it is a general YouTube editing guide (gaming, comedy sketch, music), not a Shorts/Reels breakdown. The craft rules transfer.
- **Thesis:** Cut to the essentials, carry the story with voice, hook with what/how/why, keep motion smooth with eased keyframes, and give sound design as much time as the visuals.

Rules:
1. For unscripted footage, pick only the "juiciest parts". For a structured story, cut chronologically so it is easy to follow and to voice over. [03:01–03:31]
2. Show personality through "editing characteristics": zooms, pacing and voice-over. [03:31]
3. Don't cover a long, quiet task with a music montage ("boring as hell"). Have the speaker narrate what happens and why it matters. [04:01–04:30]
4. Treat voice as the main storytelling tool. [04:30]
5. Hook in the intro by stating the **what, the how and the why** of the video. [05:02]
6. Keep intro animation "simple and smooth". Use elements that support the point (voice-over first), then add decoration. [05:31]
7. Build transitions on adjustment layers above every track so text and PNG layers transition too. A clip-only transition leaves graphics behind. [06:01]
8. Animate graphics with position, scale and rotation only. You will rarely need the other transform properties. [07:01]
9. A slide-in uses two keyframes about 1 s apart (off-screen, then home position). [07:31]
10. Shape the velocity curve so the speed is loaded at the start of the move. Front-loaded speed looks smoothest on entrances. [07:31–08:00]
11. When rotating and moving at the same time, keyframe both on the same frames and give them matching velocity shapes. The closer the curve shapes, the smoother the motion. [08:00–08:31]
12. On exit, mirror the curve used on entry. [08:31]
13. Use small "pop" subtitles: keyframed scale/position on text. [08:31–09:00]
14. Spread small touches of editing flair through the whole video, not only the intro. [09:00]
15. Give sound design the same attention as visuals. "5 minutes vs 30 minutes" of SFX work is an audible difference. [09:30]
16. Add a whoosh to every moving PNG or image: entrance, sway, point and exit ("thick air"). [10:00–10:30]
17. Adjust levels in 6 dB steps, the "sweet spot" for audible changes. [10:30]
18. Never let the meter go into the red. [10:30]
19. For overwhelming, intense dialogue, overlap lines and use J-cuts (audio leads the picture). [12:10–12:30]
20. For dramatic tension, leave space after a big line before the reaction. [13:00]
21. Letterbox with a crop of about 15 (top and bottom) on an adjustment layer. Keyframe 0→15 with ease-out/ease-in to bring the bars in on a tension moment. [13:30–14:01]
22. Use ease-in and ease-out on keyframes for smoother animation in general. [14:01]
23. For tension, use risers, slow builds and creeping ambient music. A riser signals that something big is coming. [14:30–15:02]
24. One sustained ambient note under a scene can carry the tension. [15:02]
25. Pick music to the emotion of the scene. Music is the foundation layer that sets cut pacing and tone. [16:05], [17:00]
26. Cut on the beat, on the loudest hits (snare or kick). Cuts off the beat "feel wrong". [17:30–18:00]
27. Stopping the music, then restarting it, creates a dramatic beat. [18:00]

### 9.3 How Social Media Algorithms Actually Work (And How to Beat Them)
- **Source:** 9.3 · "How Social Media Algorithms Actually Work (And How to Beat Them)" · Kallaway · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8cQidXgtGmU · 1040 s · 3,788 words · search match 0.59. Substitute: no infinite-loop video existed. **It contains no explicit loop or rewatch technique.** The relevant parts are the metrics that loops feed: average watch time in seconds, % completion and watch-time session share.
- **Thesis:** The algorithm is a matchmaker. It fingerprints the video, tests it on about 200 mostly non-followers, then scales up or stops based on watch time, completion, engagement rate and session share. To win, narrow topic and audience, and make the content relevant, non-obvious, easy to absorb and quick to act on.

Rules:
1. Platforms optimise for time on platform. Make content that keeps the matched viewer watching. [00:30–01:01]
2. Videos are analysed multimodally: computer vision on the frames, audio fingerprinting/transcript on speech, and metadata (caption, hashtag, creator, location). What is seen and said must clearly state the topic. [01:30–02:02]
3. A "fit score" predicts who will like the video. Make the topic obvious in picture and voice so the match is accurate. [02:02]
4. The first push goes to about 200 people, mostly non-followers. The video must work for a stranger with no context. [02:30–03:00]
5. Positive data scales the push roughly 10× per step (200 → 2,000 → 20,000 → 200,000). Neutral data resamples another ~200. Negative data stops it almost at once. [03:31–04:00]
6. A "200-view jail" means the first sample group gave bad data. [04:31]
7. Even hits eventually run out of matching viewers. [04:31]
8. Make videos on the same narrow topics for the same audience avatar, again and again ("audience matching"). [06:30–07:00]
9. Mixed topics give blended fit scores and weak sample data. Turn down viral ideas that would bring the wrong audience. [07:30–08:32]
10. A viral hit outside the core demo can hurt the next several videos. [08:32]
11. Sample engagement is judged on 3 core metrics: **average watch time** (seconds) and its proxy **% completion**; **engagement rate** = (likes + comments + shares) ÷ views; and **watch-time session share** (your share of the viewer's session minutes). [09:30–10:01]
12. More engagement comes from: (a) a topic relevant to the viewer's problem, (b) information that is non-obvious and usable, (c) high absorption, meaning it is easy to understand, and (d) a short distance to implement (small action, big result). [10:31–11:30]
13. In plain terms: hit a core pain point, say something useful, say it understandably, and make it applicable. [11:30–12:01]
14. The same four factors turn viewers into buyers. Include them in sales content. [12:31]
15. Choose topics from outlier videos that already work in the niche, not from guesswork. [12:31–13:00]
16. Drive comments by taking a hard stance, not by hedging. [14:00]
17. Pick the contrarian side. Disagreement drives comments. [14:00–14:31]
18. Raise the stakes in how the stance is framed (the "better than every pasta shop" level of claim). [14:31]
19. Build topics around brands, people or movements that viewers already have opinions on. [14:31–15:03]
20. Frame the take to stir strong emotion. [15:03]
21. Posting time, hashtags and caption text "don't matter". Only the video itself does. [16:01]
22. **Loop and rewatch implication (inferred, not stated):** a loop replay adds watch seconds and pushes completion past 100%, and the stated ranking inputs are watch time and completion [09:30]. A seamless loop can only help those inputs. The transcript gives no loop technique.

### 9.4 Social Media Safe Zones & Guides in Adobe Express
- **Source:** 9.4 · "Social Media Safe Zones & Guides in Adobe Express" · Darren Meredith · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xfvs-UBVfkg · 192 s · 639 words · WEAK MATCH (0.44). It is on topic (safe zones) but very thin: an Adobe Express sponsor spot with **no pixel or percentage values**. The numbers in B.5 come from the Meta Ads Guide **[NORM]**.
- **Thesis:** Platform UI covers the bottom and edges of vertical video. Turn on the platform-specific safe-zone overlay and keep captions and text inside it.

Rules:
1. Captions and on-screen text get cut off by the platform UI. Check every text element against the platform overlay. [00:00]
2. The covered area is at the bottom of Reels/Shorts/TikTok, where the like, comment and share counts sit. [00:32]
3. Use a safe-zone overlay specific to the platform. Instagram Story, Instagram Reels, Facebook Stories, Facebook Reels, TikTok and LinkedIn each differ. [00:32–01:00]
4. A LinkedIn video ad has a tighter (square-ish) safe area, so text that fits Reels may clip there. [01:00]
5. For custom destinations, add your own rulers and guides (Shift+R in Express). [01:30]
6. If text sits inside the strictest overlay you use, it works on the more lenient platforms (the example passes on TikTok inside the LinkedIn square). [02:00–02:30]
7. Move text up or inward until it sits inside the zone, rather than shrinking it to fit at the bottom. [02:30–03:02]

### 9.5 How To Make VIRAL Podcast Clip Videos! (12.5k Followers In 5 Days)
- **Source:** 9.5 · "How To Make VIRAL Podcast Clip Videos! (12.5k Followers In 5 Days)" · Joe Sbiti · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uTNnpkpYr0A · 1737 s · 8,088 words · manual replacement (match 1.0). On topic: repurposing podcast footage into 9:16 clips (a faceless compilation page in CapCut).
- **Thesis:** Find proven clips, cut the fluff, open with the hardest-hitting clip, unify the grade with an adjustment layer, keep captions small and on one line away from faces, sit the music under the voice, add a short branded end card, and export at 1080p/30.

Rules:
1. Use CapCut (app or web). The web version takes load off weak hardware. [00:30–01:01]
2. Train your feed by liking and saving niche videos, which surfaces proven ideas. [01:01–01:30]
3. Save every candidate clip somewhere you can find it again (likes, bookmarks, DMs). [01:30]
4. Identify the source by the speaker's name. Match their wardrobe and set to find the exact episode. [02:01–03:00]
5. Use a YouTube-transcript search to jump to the exact quote, not a full watch. [03:00–04:01]
6. If you screen-record, play at the highest quality so the upload stays sharp. [04:01]
7. Cut the fluff at the start, end and middle (tangents, lead-in words) so each clip gets "direct to your viewer". Example: cut the lead-in before "driven human beings" and start on those words. [05:01–05:31], [07:30–08:02]
8. Set the project to **9:16** before cutting. [05:31]
9. For letterboxed (non-full-screen) clips, scale every clip to the same size (198% in his project) so the series is consistent. Full screen is also an option. [05:31–06:01]
10. **Put the strongest, most hard-hitting clip first.** It decides whether people keep watching. [06:01–06:31]
11. A bold or confusing opening statement ("Champions are broken") is a good hook because it creates confusion and curiosity. [07:00]
12. Re-using a proven clip has trade-offs: it may be recognised, but it is proven to perform. [07:00–07:30]
13. Unify the look with one adjustment layer: brightness −5 to −10, contrast about +5, shadows down a little. [08:31]
14. A saturation choice (near black-and-white vs colour) can be a page signature. [09:00]
15. Correct individual clips on top of the global layer so none is too dark or too bright (example: global −6, one clip a further −5 = −11). [09:00–09:30]
16. Choose between auto-captions and a single static label word ("real"). Static-label videos can be done in about 10 minutes. [10:01]
17. Keep auto-captions **small**. He claims small text makes viewers look closer and pay attention. [10:01–10:31]
18. **Keep captions on one line.** Two-line captions look worse, cover more of the frame and are "annoying". Split long captions at a natural phrase break. [10:31], [11:32–12:30]
19. **Never put captions across a face.** Find one position across all clips, just below the chin and above the chest. [11:00–11:32]
20. Listen back after splitting captions to check the phrasing flows. [12:01–12:30]
21. Choose music that fits the vibe. It drives virality and follows. Test several beds against the clip. [12:30–13:01]
22. Keep music clearly below the voice but not inaudible. His bed sat at **−11 dB** for the whole video. [13:01–13:31]
23. Keep meter levels between **−12 and 0 dB** throughout, with no sections louder or quieter than the rest. [13:31–14:01]
24. If you can't meter (on a phone), match loudness to similar videos on the platform. [14:01]
25. A static label: middle of the screen, not too big, not bold, thin stroke plus shadow. [14:30]
26. Add a branded end card: logo at **25% opacity** behind a related quote for **about 5–6 s**. [15:01–16:31]
27. The end card adds originality (it protects against "unoriginal content" strikes) and a personal touch that turns viewers into followers. [15:01–15:31]
28. Fade the end card in and out. The fade-out is longer, about **1 s**. Match text and logo fades. [17:01–17:31]
29. He lets the music run past the picture onto black and fades it over **2 s** "to make it emotional". [17:31–18:01] (Conflicts with loop-friendly endings. See B.7.)
30. Do a final proof pass: caption errors, leftover fluff, uneven loudness. [18:01]
31. Export at **1080p, 30 fps**. "There's no need to do more." (The transcript reads "180p", a speech-to-text error for 1080p.) [18:30–19:00]
32. Post from the phone, not from desktop or a scheduler. He says desktop posting hurts performance. [18:30], [19:31]
33. **Instagram needs real captions.** A label-only video "tends to do a lot worse" on Instagram than one with auto-captions. [19:00–19:31]
34. Save the post as a draft and let the platform process it (up to 24 h) before posting, to avoid a low-quality encode. [19:31–20:00]
35. On TikTok, add a platform sound at volume 0 (or silence) so the platform doesn't mute the video for copyrighted music. [20:30–21:00]
36. Post captions and hashtags have little effect. "90%… is just how good your video is." [21:30–22:01]
37. Give a post 1–2 weeks before judging it. Some only take off in week 2. [22:30–23:00]
38. On TikTok, a **qualified view** means watched for **5 s or more**. About half of views qualify (53k of ~120k; about 500k per 1M). The first 5 s decide whether a view counts. [25:02–26:01]
39. RPM depends on audience geography more than niche. 89% USA gave a high RPM. [24:02]

### 9.6 Pro Tips to Reframe Horizontal Videos Perfect for Mobile (Premiere Pro)
- **Source:** 9.6 · "Pro Tips to Reframe Horizontal Videos Perfect for Mobile (Premiere Pro)" · Javier Mercedes · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1dmUYjZCCAM · 964 s · 2,640 words · manual replacement (match 1.0). On topic: reframing 16:9 to 9:16.
- **Thesis:** Auto-reframe is fine for simple material, but it misses the point of interest and outputs odd sizes. For control, frame each shot inside a 9:16 overlay on the 16:9 timeline, then change only the sequence width and export at 1080x1920.

Rules:
1. Plan for the horizontal edit to need a vertical version. Most client jobs now need both. [00:00]
2. Auto Reframe Sequence works for "easier material". [00:30–01:00], [03:01]
3. After auto-reframe, check the frame size. It comes out much smaller than the 4K source. Set it to **1080x1920** for social; 4K isn't needed. [01:00–01:31]
4. **Auto-reframe often misses the point of interest** (example: a spinning wheel left out of frame). Check every shot. [02:00]
5. Inside an auto-reframed sequence, fix framing with "reframe offset", not position. [02:00–02:30]
6. For edits you have spent days on, frame every shot by hand for vertical. [02:30–03:01]
7. Build a 9:16 boundary template on the 16:9 frame: black bars on both sides at exactly 9:16. [03:01–03:30]
8. Get exact boundaries by placing a real vertical clip with "fit to frame". This works for any source ratio (21:9, 20:9). [04:00–04:31]
9. Alternatively, drop guides on the vertical clip's edges (his example: 65.83%, pixel 2528 on a 3840 frame). [05:03–05:30]
10. Save the template as a transparent PNG (export frame), reusable on every job. [06:01–07:30]
11. Put the template on the top track at **75% opacity** so you can see what falls outside the crop while framing. Lock that track. [08:03–09:30]
12. Duplicate the sequence before converting it to vertical. [09:30–10:01]
13. Don't swap the frame size to 2160x3840 (it zooms in wrongly). Don't nest by default, and don't downscale to 1080 width (it just zooms in). [10:01–10:32]
14. Keep the height and change only the width to the 9:16 value: **width = height ÷ 16 × 9** (2160 → 1215; 1080 → 607.5). [10:32–11:32]
15. **Untick "scale motion effects proportionally"** when changing frame size, or your framing is reset and skewed. [11:32–12:30]
16. The width-only method keeps the horizontal edit's effects, transitions and framing structure. [13:01–13:30]
17. If the conversion breaks something, nest everything at 16:9 and apply the same width change to the nest. Put the template inside the nest to keep framing visible. [13:30–14:01]
18. **Don't export "Match Source"**. It gives odd sizes like 1214x2160. Set **1080x1920** explicitly, or 4K vertical if you need it. [14:01–15:00]

### 9.7 MASTER Short Form Content Editing | DaVinci Resolve 2026
- **Source:** 9.7 · "MASTER Short Form Content Editing | DaVinci Resolve 2026" · Ryan Herrick · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EEV3E2U0keQ · 780 s · 3,548 words · manual replacement (match 1.0). Partly on topic: a general Reels workflow with a short caption section (characters per line, placement, font). It contains no caption psychology (when captions help or hurt) beyond that.
- **Thesis:** Reels editing is a fast 3-step loop: waveform-driven rough cut with J-cuts and no silences, B-roll on the spoken cue, then a preset hook, captions and SFX.

Rules:
1. Set the project to vertical resolution before you edit. [00:00–00:30]
2. Cut from the waveform. Silent stretches are where the cuts go. [00:30–01:00]
3. Bind split and ripple-delete to adjacent keys for speed (slash = split, Shift+Delete = ripple delete). [01:00–02:01]
4. Rough cut first, then polish the mistakes. [02:01–02:30]
5. **J-cut every edit by about 3–4 frames**: the next clip's audio starts before its picture. [02:30–03:00]
6. **Leave no silences**: "there's always going to be talking". [03:00–03:31]
7. Start a J-cut at the beginning of a phrase, never mid-sentence. [03:31]
8. Strip audio from all B-roll in one action (link, unlink, delete). [04:00–04:30]
9. Place B-roll on the spoken cue and match the action ("drinking the can" lands on the line). Cut on the frame where the action starts. [04:30–05:01]
10. A clean look plus captions is what makes it look finished. [05:30]
11. Build or buy an asset pack (text animations, overlays, SFX). Manual building is too slow for Reels. [05:30–06:31]
12. Open on a zoom-out (a Magic Zoom-style preset) at the start of the video. [06:31–07:00]
13. Open with an on-screen hook text animation from your preset pack. [07:00]
14. Hook font choices: Apple Garamond, Gotham Ultra (both free). Font is part of what makes a hook good. [07:31]
15. Text "up" animation: keyframe centre-Y, go **20 frames** forward (the "sweet spot"), back to 0.5, ease **out cubic**. [08:00]
16. Make it look premium by dragging the ease handle to about **4 frames**, which gives a snappy arrival. [08:31]
17. Save every built text animation to a named preset bin for reuse. [08:31–09:00]
18. Mix weights within a hook line (one word bold italic, sized up), and add glow or colour to it. [09:30–10:00]
19. Use overlay assets in an "add" blend mode as transitions between clips. [10:30–11:01]
20. Punch-in or zoom on the cut into the next clip. [11:01]
21. **Auto-captions at 10 characters per line**, placed **in the middle of the frame**, font Apple Garamond Regular. [11:01–11:31]
22. Remove captions that duplicate the opening hook text. [11:01]
23. Add a dynamic zoom (slow zoom-out) on talking clips. [12:01]
24. Put a riser just before the video "comes up", at reduced level ("down to like 20"; the unit is unclear). [12:01–12:30]
25. On Instagram, use a trending audio or royalty-free music. [12:30]

### 9.8 Paddy Galloway Tells Us How To Edit YouTube For TV
- **Source:** 9.8 · "Paddy Galloway Tells Us How To Edit YouTube For TV" · The Editing Podcast · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t2M0C2tuHK8 · 2257 s · 9,160 words · manual replacement (match 1.0). On topic for packaging and first-30-seconds alignment (long-form YouTube). The opening, first-line and ending rules transfer directly. The TV-viewing rules mostly don't.
- **Thesis:** The intro is a trailer. Write the first line like a title (statement plus intrigue, built on the promise), get to the "action" fast, deliver satisfaction early, never trick the viewer, and don't let the video feel like it is ending before it ends.

Rules:
1. Videos can have 80%+ TV viewership, and TV viewers watch about 10–15% longer per video. Know the device you are editing for. [02:01–02:30]
2. Confident, premium editing earns commitment. "Insecure editing" (assuming the viewer is about to leave) reads as cheap. [02:30–04:00]
3. **On-screen text must be readable**: "if you're going to put text on screen I have to be able to read it". [04:00]
4. Don't fill space with text. Full-width text that must be read word-for-word is a failure. [04:30]
5. Keep subtitles small. Film and TV subtitles are small for a reason. [04:30]
6. Caption **timing** matters: give text enough time on screen to read. [04:30–05:02]
7. Slow the pace a little for lean-back viewing. [05:02]
8. YouTube personalises by device and time of day. [08:31–09:00]
9. Make content that 80%+ of your audience can be interested in, a "bingeable catalog" rather than random ideas. [09:30–10:01]
10. The algorithm is "stubborn". Good videos can take months to be found. [10:30–11:02]
11. Music choice matters more than ever. Custom scores are a growing option. [11:31]
12. **Script the first 30 seconds to guide the edit.** The question is "what are we saying and how does the edit get it across immediately". [12:01]
13. **Treat the intro like a thumbnail and a trailer.** Viewers preview videos in-feed before committing. [12:30–13:31]
14. In the opening seconds, put the most visually interesting thing first and the clear "here's why you should watch" information. [13:01]
15. Title is the movie title, thumbnail is the poster, intro is the trailer. [13:01–13:31]
16. **The opening 30 seconds should work like a short**: a self-contained piece that unlocks the rest. Make the trailer, put it at the front and make sure it hands over cleanly. [13:31–14:01]
17. Get the intro footage. Editors should insist creators script and shoot it. [14:31]
18. Without a scripted intro, find a storyline that builds to a high point and cut on that peak. [15:00]
19. Avoid "coming up" style teasers. Start straight in the action. [15:00]
20. Track **time to action**: seconds until the thing people clicked for (or value, for education) begins. [15:00–16:01]
21. Track **time to satisfaction**: seconds until the peak or value is delivered. [16:01]
22. Front-load something that validates the click right away. [17:01]
23. Never put the most boring step first. Put the fresh, interesting thing early. [17:01–17:30]
24. **Think about the first word.** Start with "This" or "So", which forces a statement. Never "hello and welcome". [17:30–18:00]
25. **Write the first line like a title.** [18:00]
26. Intro = **statement + intrigue**. State what it is about, building on the title rather than repeating it. [18:00–18:30]
27. Don't use cheap intrigue ("some of my choices are going to shock you"). It loses trust. [18:30–19:00]
28. Every story needs an "elephant": a large, obvious need, want, problem, peril or mystery that shows where the story is going. [19:30]
29. Put a tension line in front of the context ("I wanted to disown her" before "everyone adored her"). The same facts then become intriguing. [20:00–21:01]
30. Challenge a belief the viewer already holds in the first sentence ("you probably think X; actually…"). [22:00]
31. Read retention-chart *shape* rather than one percentage. An early drop followed by a flat hold is healthy. [24:01]
32. Text that appears and must be read during a key beat can cause a drop, but don't over-read a single dip. [24:30–25:01]
33. Right after you deliver a payoff, viewers feel "done". Signpost that more is coming ("the first of a few things") without overt "keep watching" lines. [25:30–26:31]
34. Don't break immersion with a sudden CTA mid-hold. Place CTAs about 10–20 s after a payoff (the "danger zone"), subtly. [27:01–27:30]
35. Be slower in the body when the viewer is waiting for a promised thing, and be fast in the transitions. [28:00]
36. Enter scenes as late as possible and exit as early as possible. The fluff lives at entries and exits. [28:00–28:30]
37. **Don't leave a long resolution after the climax. Viewers leave the moment the video feels like it is ending.** [29:02–30:31]
38. A cliffhanger works on TV but rarely on YouTube. Every upload is someone's first video, so each one must stand alone. [32:30–37:00]

### B.2 Short-form ad translation (9:16, 30–60 s, talking-head Meta Reels ad)

- **Safe zone (rule 1).** Meta's Reels overlay covers the profile name, the "Sponsored" label, the CTA button ("Learn more" / "Send WhatsApp message"), the caption text and the side engagement rail. On 1080x1920 text lives inside **x 65–1015, y 269–1248**. A selfie usually has the face in the y≈400–900 band, so captions belong just under the chin (9.5 [11:32]) and **must end above y=1248**. The house rule "mid-chest" (EDIT-STANDARD §2) is only valid if mid-chest is above 1248 px; on a close selfie it often isn't. The right-side engagement rail sits inside the bottom-right; the 6% side margin is the published number, so keep important words away from the right edge in the lower half too.
- **Opening (rules 2, 3, 12).** The ad's first line is its title (9.8 [18:00]): "This…" / "So…" / a direct problem statement for the local-business owner ("Your listing is losing you calls"). No "Hi, I'm…" opener. Meta counts a 3-second video view **[NORM: Meta 3-second video views metric]**, and TikTok counts a qualified view at 5 s (9.5 [25:02]). So the problem and promise must land by about 3 s, and the offer or "action" should start by about 5–8 s **[DERIVED]**. 9.8's "first 30 s is a short" scales down to: *the first 3–5 s is the trailer for the remaining 25–55 s.*
- **Captions (rule 4).** Instagram punishes caption-less clips (9.5 [19:00]), and most Reels are watched with sound off **[NORM: widely cited Meta guidance to design for sound-off]**. Captions are non-negotiable. 9.7's 10 characters per line is a pop-caption style. 9.5's "one line" and 9.8's "small, readable, timed" fit the default caption style (small, white, sentence case).
- **Pacing (rule 5).** Fits the house pause gate (≤ 0.25 s) directly. 9.7's "no silences, ever" matches it.
- **Ending (rule 6) is the main adaptation.** Organic advice says don't signal the end (9.8) and the format loops (9.1). A paid ad, though, carries a CTA button and needs the brand sign-off (house "Ending" gate). **Translation:** the sign-off is the last spoken line. Cut on its last syllable plus a few frames. No fade to black, no music tail on black (reject 9.5 [17:31]). Hold any end card ≤ 2–3 s. The last frame should cut back cleanly to frame 0 on auto-replay (same framing family, no black frame) so a rewatch reads as a loop and not a restart.
- **Mid-video CTA timing (rule 22).** In a 30–60 s ad there is no "10–20 s after the payoff" window. Use one explicit CTA in the last 3–5 s, plus a subtle signpost after the first proof point ("and that's just the first thing").
- **Reframing (rule 13).** The selfie A-roll is shot native vertical, so most of 9.6 doesn't apply. It applies only when 16:9 B-roll or client footage is cut into the ad: use the width = height × 9/16 maths and hand-framing on the point of interest.
- **Rules that do NOT transfer:**
  - 9.8 TV viewing: slow the pace, longer videos, smaller subtitles for 60-inch screens [02:01–05:31]. Reels ads are phone-only and lean-forward.
  - 9.8 episodic and cliffhanger structure [32:30–37:00]. Each ad is a standalone entry point.
  - 9.2 letterbox bars [13:30]. They waste vertical pixels and fight the UI overlays.
  - 9.5 letterboxed clips at 198% with black top and bottom [05:31–06:01]. That is a faceless-page style. Full-bleed suits a talking head.
  - 9.5 TikTok-specific tactics: a muted platform sound, the Creativity Program, posting from the phone and drafts for 24 h [18:30–21:00]. Paid Meta ads are uploaded to Ads Manager, so none of these apply, though "let processing finish and preview the ad on a phone" still does.
  - 9.1 monetisation and RPM, and 9.3 hashtags, posting time and audience-matching across a channel. These are campaign and organic concerns, not edit decisions.
  - 9.3 comment-bait via contrarian or cult-brand stances [14:00–15:03]. Only a mild "hard stance" on the client's own category is brand-safe for a local business.
  - 9.7 trending audio [12:30]. Paid ads must use licensed or royalty-free music; trending audio is not available in Ads Manager the same way.

### B.4 QA (senior-editor questions while watching)

1. **"If I saw only the first 3 seconds, do I know who this is for and what problem it solves?"** Good: a named problem or claim in the first sentence. The face and the first caption appear on frame 1, with no logo sting or greeting first. [9.8 [17:30–18:30], 9.5 [06:01]]
2. **"Is the first word pushing into a sentence?"** Good: "This…", "So…", "Your…", a number. Bad: "Hi", "Hello", "Today". [9.8 [17:30–18:00]]
3. **"Does the intrigue feel earned or cheap?"** Good: a real tension, such as the "elephant" (a lost customer, a hidden ranking problem). Bad: "you won't believe…". [9.8 [18:30–20:00]]
4. **"Can I read every caption without pausing?"** Good: each caption chunk is gone only after I've read it comfortably, and nothing sits under the CTA button. [9.8 [04:00–05:02]]
5. **"Does any text fight the face?"** Good: captions sit in a constant position below the chin and never cross the mouth or eyes. [9.5 [11:00–11:32]]
6. **"Is anything slack?"** Good: every line starts on a meaningful word; no breaths or "so, um" lead-ins. [9.5 [07:30], 9.7 [03:00]]
7. **"When does the video feel over, and is that the real end?"** Good: the feeling of ending arrives with the sign-off and no earlier. No long dwell after the CTA. [9.8 [29:02–30:31]]
8. **"On replay, does the loop jar?"** Good: last frame to first frame feels like a cut, not a crash to black. [9.1 [08:58]]
9. **"Would a stranger with no context follow it?"** Good: the business type, place and offer are said and shown. [9.3 [02:30–03:00]]
10. **"Is the music supporting or competing?"** Good: you notice the music only when you listen for it, and the voice is always intelligible. [9.5 [13:01]]
11. **"Do the moves feel smooth and motivated?"** Good: eased entries, mirrored exits, sound on every move, no linear slides. [9.2 [07:31–10:30]]
12. **"Is the look consistent from shot to shot?"** Good: no brightness or colour jumps between takes. [9.5 [09:00–09:30]]
13. **"Does it look native to Reels, not like a cropped YouTube video?"** Good: subject well framed for 9:16, no letterbox bars, no dead space. [9.1 [23:32–24:31], 9.6 [02:00]]

### B.7 Conflicts & open questions (and my call)

1. **Caption position: EDIT-STANDARD "mid-chest" vs the Meta safe zone vs 9.7 "middle of frame" vs 9.5 "below chin, above chest".** On a 1080x1920 selfie, mid-chest often falls below y=1248, inside Meta's bottom 35%. 9.7's centre of frame (y≈960) risks crossing the face on a close shot. **Call:** the anchor is "just below the chin" (9.5) and is clamped to y2 ≤ 1248 and to no overlap with the face box. Proposed amendment to EDIT-STANDARD §2: "mid-chest **or higher, never below y = 1248 px**".
2. **Text safe area: EDIT-STANDARD "inside a 60 px safe area" vs Meta's 14/35/6%.** 60 px matches the 6% side margin (65 px) but is far too small at the top (269 px) and bottom (672 px). **Call:** replace the house Text gate with Q1 (x 65–1015, y 269–1248) for all text, logos, prices and CTAs. Keep 60 px only for decorative graphics that may bleed.
3. **Lines per caption: EDIT-STANDARD "up to 2 lines" vs 9.5 "never two lines" vs 9.7 "10 chars/line".** **Call:** keep the house limit of 2 lines as the hard gate, make 1 line the target (warn on 2), and cap a line at 32 chars. 9.7's 10-char pop captions clash with the house's calm small-caption look, so I reject them as a style but accept them as evidence that short lines read better.
4. **Caption size: 9.5 "really small" / 9.8 "subtitles small" vs readability on a phone.** These agree with the house ("small, white"). **Open:** no source gives a px size. Proposal **[DERIVED]**: cap height ≥ 40 px at 1080 width (about 3.7% of frame width) so it stays readable on a 6-inch phone. Needs a phone test.
5. **Ending: 9.5 (5–6 s logo/quote card, 2 s music fade on black) vs 9.8 (viewers leave the moment it feels over) vs loop format (9.1) vs house "Ending" gate (full sign-off).** **Call:** keep the complete spoken sign-off (house). No fade to black, no music on black. End card ≤ 2–3 s inside the safe zone, cut cleanly so the replay restarts without a black flash (Q15–Q18). The 9.5 tail is a faceless-page TikTok habit tied to the Creativity Program and does not transfer.
6. **Music level: 9.5 (bed at −11 dB, meters −12…0 dB) vs house (bed ≈ −7 LU under the voice; the user found −10/−14/−23 too quiet).** These aren't the same scale. 9.5's −11 dB is a clip-gain setting on a pre-mastered track and 0 dB peaks would break the house −1 dBTP. **Call:** the house rule wins (−7 LU plus band-split ducking, −14 LUFS master, TP ≤ −1 dBTP). 9.5's useful part is steadiness, enforced as Q24.
7. **Export: 9.5 (1080p/30) and 9.6 (1080x1920) vs the Meta Ads Guide recommendation (1440x2560 for Reels ads, reported).** **Call:** deliver 1080x1920 as the brief specifies. It is within Meta's spec. Optionally produce a 1440x2560 upscale-free master only if the source is 4K. Never upscale a 1080 source to 1440 (no gain, extra encode).
8. **Frame rate: 9.5 says 30 fps.** The house pipeline renders at the source rate. **Call:** keep the source rate (no 25↔30 conversion, which judders). Accept 30 fps when the source is 30.
9. **Hook length: no transcript gives a seconds figure for short-form.** 9.8's "first 30 s = a short" is about long-form; 9.5's 5 s qualified view is a TikTok payout rule. **Call:** first word ≤ 0.25 s, problem or promise complete by 3 s (Meta 3-s view **[NORM]**), offer or value by ≤ 8 s **[DERIVED]**. These are gates to tune against Ads Manager hold rates (3-s views ÷ impressions, ThruPlays) once data exists.
10. **J-cuts (9.7, 3–4 frames on every edit) vs the house rule that every cutaway snaps to an exact segment boundary.** They don't actually conflict: J-cuts apply to audio leading picture on A-roll speech joins, and the snapping rule is about the picture boundary of graphics. **Call:** allow a 2–4 frame audio lead on A-roll joins only, never a picture flash of the next take.
11. **Cut to the beat (9.2) vs cut on speech.** On a talking-head ad, A-roll cuts follow words. **Call:** beat-sync only cutaway entrances and graphic hits, within ±1 frame of a detected onset, and never move a speech cut to hit a beat.
12. **Comment-bait (9.3 contrarian, cult brands) vs local-business brand safety.** **Call:** allow a firm stance about the problem ("most listings are set up wrong"), never about named competitors or contested topics.
13. **"Captions don't matter" (9.3 [16:01], 9.5 [21:30]).** This refers to the *post caption text*, not burned-in subtitles. There is no conflict with rule 4. Note this so nobody mis-applies it.
14. **Loop technique remains an open question.** No source in this module teaches a seamless loop (9.3 was a substitute). The loop gates (Q15–Q18) are my derivations from 9.8's "don't signal the end" and the completion metric in 9.3. Validate them by A/B testing a hard-cut ending vs an end-card ending on the same ad, comparing average watch time and ThruPlay rate.
15. **The Meta safe-zone px values are conversions of Meta's published percentages.** Meta publishes only percentages; 269/672/65 px are 14/35/6% of 1920/1920/1080. Meta's own guidance on the Reels ads pages also says 1440x2560 is recommended. Re-check the Ads Manager placement preview when Meta changes the overlay.
