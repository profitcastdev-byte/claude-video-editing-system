### 4.1 Give me 15 mins, and I'll make your hooks impossible to skip
- **Source:** 4.1 | Kallaway | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2byPP_9F0-Q | 948 s (15:48) | ~3,620 words | direct match (1.0). Two long ad reads ([07:01]–[08:32], [15:01]–[15:31]) contain no technique.
- **Thesis:** A hook has one job, to make the viewer opt in, and it does that by delivering topic clarity plus on-target curiosity. Hooks fail for only four reasons: delay, confusion, irrelevance, disinterest.

**Rules**
1. Treat the hook's single job as getting the viewer to opt in and stay; it must stop the scroll and keep them put. [00:30]
2. Make every hook deliver exactly two things: **topic clarity** (viewer knows precisely what the video is about) and **on-target curiosity** (viewer believes it is for them, will give value, and wonders what comes next). [00:30–01:01]
3. Diagnose a failing hook against the four mistakes in order: delay, confusion, irrelevance, disinterest. [01:30, 15:01]
4. **Fix delay first (it is the easiest).** Delay = the context of what the video is about arrives too late. [01:30]
5. If the topic first appears in, e.g., line 3 of a 13-line script, delete lines 1–2 as fluff and move line 3 to the top. [01:30]
6. Introduce the topic within the **first 1–2 seconds** ("speed to value": shorten the time until the viewer reaches clarity/value). [02:00]
7. Anti-pattern: suspense hooks with zero context ("one of the craziest things I've ever seen…"). They feel suspenseful but give the viewer nothing to decide on, because "a crazy thing" could be anything. [02:00–02:31]
8. Remember the short-form retention curve is exponential decay, steepest in the **first two seconds**; every second without telling the viewer the topic loses a large share of viewers. [02:31–03:02]
9. Where vague hooks do work, it is because of the creator's looks, facial emotion, or the **on-screen text hook**, not the spoken words; so if you must be vague in speech, the text and face are carrying the hook. [03:02]
10. Use the first sentence, in as few words as possible, to give the context needed to opt in (e.g. "Here are three simple ways to improve your gut health"). [03:30–04:01]
11. **Fix confusion** (= comprehension loss): the topic is there but wording/phrasing is unclear, so the viewer cannot judge whether the video is for them. [04:01–04:31]
12. Re-order the sentence so the idea lands in reading order (confusing "$30M empire… most difficult to earn if you don't develop a journaling practice" vs clear "…their secret for earning money online was their insane journaling practice"). [04:31–05:31]
13. Use fewer words, but enough that the viewer cannot misunderstand. [05:31]
14. Write at a **sixth-grade reading level**. [05:31]
15. Use direct active voice, not passive ("The dog jumped" not "the jump of the dog"). [05:31]
16. Optional tool: give an LLM the hook and ask it to keep the meaning identical but rewrite for clarity at a sixth-grade level. [06:00]
17. Read the 1–2 hook sentences in isolation and ask: could this be misunderstood, or read more than one way? If yes, rewrite to remove every alternative interpretation. [06:30]
18. Treat hooks as the biggest single lever ("the 80 of the 80/20"). [07:01]
19. **Fix irrelevance:** viewer knows the topic but is not confident it is valuable for them. [08:32–09:00]
20. Say **"you/your" instead of "I/me/my"** in the hook ("If you've struggled with skin problems…" not "I've struggled with skin problems…"); first-person invites the viewer to question whether they are like the creator. [09:00–10:00]
21. Frame the hook around **expected value**: entertainment solves boredom, education solves a specific problem; agitate a pain point the viewer already has, then tee up the solution. [10:00–10:30]
22. Prefer need-to-have pain over nice-to-know trivia ("If you struggle with acne, try these three things" beats "three common trends in skincare"). [10:30]
23. **Fix disinterest** (the curiosity problem) by opening a curiosity loop. [11:01]
24. Understand a curiosity loop: viewer sees something → asks a question → gets context → which spurs a new question → more context…, repeated through the whole video. The hook's job is to open the first loop. [11:01–11:31]
25. Generate curiosity with **contrast/comparison**: the distance between what the viewer currently believes (A) and your alternative (B) (e.g. "clear acne in 45 minutes, no side effects" vs the known 6 months of Accutane). [12:01–12:32]
26. Make B solve the pain **faster, better or cheaper** than A; the comparison re-agitates the pain A has not solved. [12:32–13:01]
27. Use **stated contrast** (say both A and B, e.g. "Most people use Accutane, but this herbal remedy is three times faster") when you want it impossible to miss. [13:01–13:30]
28. Use **implied contrast** (say only B, e.g. "this supplement is eight times more effective") when the baseline is common knowledge. [13:30–14:01]
29. Structure: topic clarity in the first sentence, contrast in the next 1–2 sentences; a hook is usually **2–3 lines**. A one-line hook that does both is the most reusable. [14:01–14:30]

Rule count: 29.

### 4.2 How to Become a Storytelling Genius (Dopamine Ladders)
- **Source:** 4.2 | Kallaway | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jtmstMt4WLc | 1,345 s (22:25) | ~5,100 words | direct match (1.0). Ad read at [21:30]–[22:00].
- **Thesis:** Viewers climb six "rungs" of rising reward: stimulation, captivation, anticipation, validation (the message), then affection and revelation (the messenger). Engineer each rung deliberately; levels 1–4 live inside a single video.

**Rules**
1. Take the viewer through six stages in order; reaching all six means they watched to the end and want more. [00:31]
2. The end goal is a conditioned ("Pavlovian") response: the viewer feels reward on seeing your name or face alone. [01:00–01:31]
3. **Level 1, Stimulation**, happens in the **first 1–2 seconds**, and is the same for short and long form. [02:00]
4. Stimulate with **colour, motion, brightness and contrast that differ from what the viewer normally sees** in the feed. It is a "visual stun gun". [02:00–02:30]
5. Know that eyes go first to the frames with more motion, colour and brightness (blurred 9:16 grid demo); feeds watched in greyscale feel less interesting. [02:30–03:00]
6. Bottom-up visual processing happens in about **200 ms**: the eye catches vivid colour and motion before comprehension. [03:00–03:30]
7. Put enough colour, motion and contrast in the first 1–2 s to stun the viewer into a pause. [03:30]
8. Do not copy the same motion/colour pattern as everyone else: the feed desensitises viewers to it. Build your **own colour palette, motion and aesthetic** to cut through. [03:30–04:00]
9. Stimulation is the weakest, shortest reward, but nothing else matters without it. [04:00]
10. **Level 2, Captivation**: now what you say and how you say it matters; the viewer must form an open question in their own head (a curiosity loop). [04:00–04:30]
11. The bigger and less obvious the question, and the higher the stakes, the more curiosity; the more relevant the question to the viewer, the more curiosity. [04:30–05:00]
12. A huge question the viewer does not care about (aliens example) still causes churn: relevance gates curiosity. [05:00]
13. When writing the hook ask: what is the most curiosity-inducing question about this topic, and how well does it map to the ideal viewer? Then pick the words **and visuals** that make that question pop. [05:31]
14. Plant the question either by asking it rhetorically or by implying it with details; either way, as early as possible. [05:31–06:01]
15. Stimulation without relevant captivation still loses the viewer (makeup-tutorial example: stopped by visuals, churned on topic). [06:01–07:01]
16. If hooks do not hook, the question that popped is either irrelevant or not interesting enough. [07:01]
17. Pop bigger curiosity by contrasting a known thing with an unknown one, or by framing a shocking/weird premise never seen before; unknowns pop curiosity best. [07:01–07:30]
18. **Level 3, Anticipation**: the viewer starts guessing the answer, like a mystery playing out in real time. [07:30–08:00]
19. Feed details that bring the viewer closer to the answer; curiosity rises up to the reveal, and the peak is **just before the answer**. [08:00]
20. Just before delivering the answer, head-fake: misdirection, non-obvious twists, or a turn in another direction reset the loop. [08:00–08:30]
21. Alternatively, give the answer and immediately pop a second question. [08:30]
22. Viewers can only anticipate if they are clear on the facts; rogue or confusing details make them bounce. [09:00]
23. Plan anticipation: what should they expect the answer to be, what facts get them closer, and should you answer or head-fake now? [09:00–09:30]
24. Visual anticipation hooks work too (a car slowly approaching a person: "will they get hit?"). [09:30–10:00]
25. **Level 4, Validation**: close the loop with an answer that is non-obvious, something they did not expect. [10:30]
26. For entertainment, resolve the story; do not end on a cliffhanger. For education, deliver the concrete tip/recommendation that solves the problem. [10:30–11:01]
27. If the loop is left open, the viewer leaves unsatisfied and the final reward never happens. [11:01]
28. Levels 1–4 are about the message; levels 5–6 are about the messenger. [11:30–12:01]
29. **Level 5, Affection**: viewers who like the presenter give a longer leash; faceless content struggles to get here. [12:01–12:31]
30. Likability lever 1: attractiveness. If the presenter is good-looking, show them more. [12:31–13:00]
31. Lever 2: vibe (dress, posture, how they sit and carry themselves). [13:00]
32. Lever 3: smiling; joy transfers through the lens. [13:00–13:31]
33. Lever 4 (the strongest): actually solve the viewer's problem. [13:31]
34. Do not force likability; be authentically passionate. [14:00]
35. **Level 6, Revelation**: the viewer realises you will keep delivering value. It is easier to reach with education than entertainment. [15:00–15:30]
36. Reach revelation by delivering non-obvious, differentiated value repeatedly; the deeper the viewer's pain, the fewer videos it takes. [16:31–17:00]
37. Worked example, stimulation: a slight zoom-in at the very start plus an unusual camera perspective is enough. [17:30–18:01]
38. Worked example, captivation: in the first 2–4 s, on-screen text ("Bro, what did I just witness?") re-opened a question just as the viewer was about to churn. [18:01–18:30]
39. Three or four rapid questions in succession push curiosity high. [18:30]
40. Worked example, validation: a hard match cut to an unexpected answer (he is dragged, singing), with the audio coming in on the cut, closes the loop and head-fakes at once. [19:01–19:31]
41. Use the ladder as a checklist when reviewing an edit, and reverse-engineer content that held your own attention. [21:00–21:30]

Rule count: 41.

### 4.3 How To Edit SO GOOD That People Can't Stop Watching
- **Source:** 4.3 | Gavin Herman | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C3U1YGwLopU | 525 s (8:45) | ~1,840 words | direct match (1.0). Sponsor reads at [02:01]–[03:01] and [08:00]–[08:30].
- **Thesis:** Five editing rules: control pace like a storyteller, make every medium belong, make every frame beautiful, make motion serve a purpose, and treat sound design as the glue.

**Rules**
1. Decide pacing deliberately for each video: fast and punchy, or slowed to breathe. [00:00–00:30]
2. The social-media default works: rapid fire, no silences, no breaths, as much information as possible in the shortest time. [00:30]
3. Fast is not automatically better; it is not one-size-fits-all. [01:01]
4. Tutorials: fast enough to keep engagement, slow enough to actually teach clearly. [01:01–01:31]
5. Dense-wisdom content: slow down so viewers can digest or take notes. [01:31]
6. Fast entertainment content should still slow down for serious or emotional moments; breaking the expected rhythm is a **pattern break**. [01:31]
7. Blend mediums: camera footage, 2D motion graphics, 3D, stock, screenshots, AI-generated assets. [02:01]
8. Randomly mixing assets is not skill; make every asset feel like it belongs, by matching colours and lighting. [03:01]
9. Motion-track inserted elements so they live inside the shot instead of sitting on top as a basic overlay. [03:01]
10. Pause anywhere: the frame should look good, as if you spent an hour on it. [03:30]
11. Match typography to the background colours so the frame is one cohesive scene; viewers judge visual quality subconsciously at all times. Think like a designer. [04:01]
12. Design principle, alignment: line up edges or centres to create order. [04:01–04:30]
13. Hierarchy: the most important element stands out first and guides the eye. [04:30]
14. Contrast: differences in colour, size, shape or style separate elements. [04:30]
15. Repetition: reuse colours, fonts, shapes for unity. [04:30]
16. Proximity: group related elements so they read as connected. [04:30]
17. Balance: distribute visual weight evenly. [04:30]
18. Colour: choose hues to set mood, create harmony and draw attention. [04:30–05:00]
19. Emphasis: make one element the focal point. [05:00]
20. Apply design to camera shots too: contrasting background vs wardrobe, slight background blur, a light on the head so the subject pops, crop to rebalance an uneven shot. [05:00–05:31]
21. Motion graphics are a tool, not decoration; do not copy a style because it looks cool. [05:31–06:00]
22. Every animation must either make an idea easier to understand or make the story more engaging. [06:00]
23. Reveal each item visually as it is described (layers of the earth, one by one). [06:00]
24. Turn complex ideas into a simple animated chart. [06:00]
25. Use kinetic type for high energy; use transitions to bridge moments and replace awkward cuts. [06:00–06:31]
26. Animations that neither clarify nor enhance are noise. [06:31]
27. Sound design is the glue: a well-paced edit falls flat if audio does not match its energy. [06:31]
28. Vocals must be crisp, with minimal reverb and background noise; use a denoise/de-reverb tool when the room is poor. [06:31–07:30]
29. Use music and SFX for immersion. [07:30]
30. Balance the mix: the classic beginner mistake is music or SFX so loud the speaker cannot be heard. [07:30–08:00]
31. Sync every SFX to its visual; adjust its speed/length to match (door shut example). Off-sync SFX breaks the viewer's flow. [08:00]
32. Layer several sounds to make a scene more immersive. [08:00]
33. Viewers forgive bad visuals but click away instantly from bad audio. [08:00]

Rule count: 33.

### 4.4 The Retention Editing Playbook (Asendify)
- **Source:** 4.4 | Asendify | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rMTsUrMnnWc | 137 s (2:17) | ~380 words | **weak match** (search, 0.57).
- **Judgement:** This is a sales ad for a paid 38-page PDF (£600), not a lesson. It lists chapter headings but teaches none of them. It is on-topic only as a table of contents of what a retention system covers; nothing here is a usable rule on its own. Its value is as a coverage checklist: every heading below is covered with substance elsewhere in this module or should be covered by another module.
- **Thesis:** Retention is the growth metric; diagnose where viewers leave, then fix the hook, structure, pacing and sound.

**Rules (headings only, unsubstantiated in the video)**
1. Treat retention as the metric that grows a channel; early exits are the leak to fix. [00:00]
2. Diagnose **where** viewers leave from the retention graph (it claims four graph shapes and ten common leaks; none are named). [00:32]
3. Treat the first 30 s as its own unit with a **promise, proof, path** hook structure (not explained); avoid "seven intro killers" (not named). [00:32]
4. Plan open loops and re-hooks across the structure. [01:01]
5. Schedule pattern interrupts ("pattern interrupts on a schedule"). [01:01]
6. Treat captions, on-screen text and sound as retention tools; run a pre-export checklist. [01:01]
7. Fix the drop "starting with second one". [02:01]

Rule count: 7 (all headings; low evidential weight). The "promise, proof, path" idea is consistent with 4.1 (clarity/relevance) and 4.6 (proof beats promise).

### 4.5 How Logan Paul's Best Video Was Made | Editing Breakdown
- **Source:** 4.5 | Hayden Hillier-Smith (HillierSmith) | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2v-Dv5YYaGQ | 809 s (13:29) | ~2,220 words | direct match (1.0). Long-form documentary vlog; principles transfer, durations do not.
- **Thesis:** Retention comes from contrast and variety (sound, rhythm, pace) used to steer emotion along a goal line, from withholding the key image to build anticipation, and from building everything toward one "zenith" moment that is then left alone.

**Rules**
1. A single shot can run **over 17 seconds without a cut** and keep high retention if the story has earned it. [00:00]
2. **Contrast editing for attention:** leave talking heads "naked" (no music, no SFX), cut to a high-intensity cutaway with multiple layers of SFX, and at the peak of that energy cut back to the naked talking head. [00:30]
3. Repeat the contrast to lock it in; contrast creates focus. [01:01]
4. Once you have focus, open a mystery. [01:01]
5. Let a key word in the script set the cutting style: "rare" = elusive, sneaky, sparse, so cuts to the subject's photos are so fast you barely see them, and the card is obscured by headlines of its value. [01:01–01:30]
6. **Tease and withhold** the real image: show an animated stand-in instead of the real thing to build anticipation. [01:30]
7. Prefer **story-showing over storytelling**: visually represent what the speaker says rather than cutting back to him saying it. [02:00]
8. Interpret lines literally when it clarifies: he says "20 of them", show 20; "higher value", throw in a money animation; he mentions a population chart, show the chart. [02:31]
9. Extreme close-ups with fast cuts, plus a zoom and glow around the object, signal obsession and desire (borrowed from *A Clockwork Orange*). [03:01]
10. Withhold the talking head for an extended stretch (here a full minute) so that when you return to it, viewers know the line is important. [03:32]
11. Use the A-roll as **bookends** to a B-roll sequence. [03:32]
12. Use stylistic references from the subject's own world (Pokémon game/TV visuals) to keep the object mythic and out of reach. [04:00]
13. Keep every cut to the key object, across the whole 2-minute opening, consistent with the withholding idea. [04:00–04:31]
14. After a long aggressive fast-paced sequence reaches its climax, **change rhythm**: slow down and keep the pauses. Keeping the same fast rhythm "would get exhausting quickly". [04:31–05:00]
15. Variety itself is refreshing because it differs from what came before. [05:00]
16. Escalate into medium pace with medium-paced music for a rising sequence. [05:00–05:31]
17. Compress long real time (2+ hours of phone negotiations) to only the most informative progression lines, with aggressive time skips that move on before the viewer settles. [05:31]
18. Escalate the music on each beat to build anticipation. [05:31–06:00]
19. On a setback, switch to prolonged (slower) rhythmic cuts and matching music; **rhythmic variety tells the viewer how to feel**. [06:00]
20. Model the emotional journey as a line toward a goal: rise, near-miss fall ("pit of despair"), climb back. [06:31–07:01]
21. After a fall, remind viewers of the goal. [07:01]
22. Music choice sets tone; a "serious" track on a light subject can heighten it. [07:31]
23. When story-changing information lands, build the music and then **break** it; the break highlights the beat. [07:31–08:00]
24. Hold on the shot of a character making a choice, swell the music, then reward the choice instantly: big music swell, best shots, rhythm switching to fast. A change in the narrative is rewarded with variety. [08:00–08:31]
25. Deliver the withheld payoff (the real card) only after it has been teased all video. [08:31]
26. Use a zoom-out reveal as a visual storytelling transition (assumed Italy, revealed Dubai). [09:00]
27. Characterise mystery with curious, tense music, out-of-focus footage and dialogue that affirms the mystery. [09:00–09:30]
28. Lay dialogue under B-roll. [09:30]
29. Hold on a reveal moment (a case opening) for the reaction; let the question "what's in it?" drive the next sequence. [09:30–10:00]
30. **Hyper-focus on the theme**: from an hour of footage, use only the parts about the central object. [10:00]
31. **Rule of three**: set up the pattern with one, confirm it with a second, so the viewer anticipates the third. [10:32]
32. The "zenith cut": make the whole edit build toward one single moment that captures everything. [11:00]
33. At the zenith, do almost nothing; let it play. Slow, quiet cuts there had the highest retention in the video. [11:30–12:05]
34. Follow the peak's silence or bewilderment with the big music cue. [12:05–12:34]
35. Emphasise a peak by removing ambient audio, cutting to slow motion and fading into a symbolic image. [13:01]

Rule count: 35.

### 4.6 Every viral RETENTION TACTIC explained (with examples)
- **Source:** 4.6 | Learn By Leo | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ooWMvH3341k | 1,081 s (18:01) | ~3,230 words | search match (1.0). Mostly YouTube long-form story/challenge examples; plugs at [02:01] and [17:02].
- **Thesis:** Eighteen tactics, all of them ways to keep the viewer constantly clear about what they would miss by leaving: front-loaded proof, enthusiasm, open questions, conflict, stakes, mystery, progression and clarity.

**Rules**
1. Put the **most extraordinary thing in the video as close to the start as possible**, so the video proves it is special before anyone thinks of leaving. [00:00]
2. Anti-pattern: saving the best for last with "watch till the end to see X"; only about 20% of viewers reach the end. [00:00–00:30]
3. **Proof beats promise:** open with the most attention-grabbing thing you have. [00:30]
4. Speak with **infectious enthusiasm**; it signals you have something special and builds trust even for an unknown creator (8M-view example). [01:01–02:01]
5. Never let the viewer feel they know enough: before explaining something, first tell them what they are missing by not knowing it. [02:01–02:30]
6. Retention in one sentence: keep the viewer constantly clear about **what they will miss if they stop watching**. [02:30–03:01]
7. Use a fact → question → fact cadence so the viewer never feels done. [03:01]
8. Introduce an antagonist for a constant threat of failure and ongoing conflict. [03:01–04:01]
9. Make parts without conflict go by as fast as possible (a year of planning plus building summarised in a ~20–30 s montage). [04:01–04:32]
10. Open with the **most broadly appealing question**, the one everyone is already asking ("What is using it actually like?"). [05:00–05:31]
11. Promise both the incredible details and the bad stuff. [05:31]
12. Name a **common pain point before solving it**: show "I understand your problem". [05:31–06:31]
13. Use pain points throughout the video, not only in the intro. [06:31]
14. Add a **time limit** for urgency and threat of failure. [06:31–07:30]
15. Develop a **mystery**: introduce something without showing it, then call it back later (here 6 minutes later). [07:30–08:31]
16. In the intro, lead with the most intriguing, exceptional details ("feet in shark-infested waters with a bucket of blood"). [08:31]
17. **Share the stakes** and why they matter to the person. [08:31–09:31]
18. Stakes need not be money (a silly bet works). [09:31]
19. If things get stale, introduce a new challenge or objective. [09:31–10:31]
20. Follow the natural shifts of tone in the material and amplify them with **music choice, clip duration and sound design**; switch music track so the music always matches the moment's mood. [10:31–11:30]
21. Give the viewer something to look forward to with **clear progression**: start with the least impressive and escalate (or a top-10, or rising difficulty). [11:30–12:01]
22. Say what might go wrong and what is lost if it does, to maximise suspense. [12:01–13:00]
23. Keep **perfect clarity** about what is happening; add a voiceover to clarify where we are in the story. [13:00–14:01]
24. Make the video constantly more interesting (progressively removing more senses). [14:01]
25. Add competition (people vs people, or thing vs thing). [14:01–15:01]
26. Highlight something **counterintuitive or surprising**; it demands an explanation and is the best conversation starter. [15:01–15:31]
27. Compress a long story to a highlight reel of its best moments (months across countries into 9 minutes). [15:31–16:31]
28. A goal plus an unexpected challenge creates the question "is this even possible?"; this needs cleverness, not budget. [16:31–17:02]

Rule count: 28.

### 4.7 The Terrible Truth About Retention Editing
- **Source:** 4.7 | Cutting Concepts | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nlWcTZwKsas | 322 s (5:22) | ~890 words | direct match (1.0).
- **Thesis:** Retention editing (loud, flashy, constant novelty) works, but "still watching" is not "enjoying". Without character, story and emotion it produces hollow, forgettable videos. Use it as a tool, not a substitute.

**Rules**
1. Retention = the percentage of the video watched, averaged over views; retention editing aims to maximise it to the end. [00:00]
2. The style relies on loud noises, flashing lights and a continuous stream of novelty; it works because brains are wired to attend to these. [00:32–01:00]
3. Benchmark cited: YouTube giants using these techniques retain **over 70%** of their audience; the average video retains **about 30–40%**. [01:00–01:30]
4. Do not mistake "still watching" for "entertained"; that is a causal fallacy. Psychological quirks can hold viewers who are not enjoying it. [01:30–02:02]
5. Techniques are style, not substance; character, story and emotion are the backbone. [02:02]
6. Anti-pattern: **manufactured conflict**, a threat that takes ~15 s to set up, is resolved in one cut with no character agency, and has no long-term relevance. [02:02–03:02]
7. Over-stimulation comes from not trusting the viewer; it leaves the experience hollow and forgettable. [03:02]
8. Platforms reward retention because it is an advertising metric, which pushes cookie-cutter, homogenised content. [03:31–04:00]
9. Retention editing is a tool, neither good nor bad; it gets misused because it feels safe. [04:00–04:31]
10. Working alternatives: put story first, or fold retention techniques into character-based concepts. [04:31–05:00]

Rule count: 10.

### 4.8 Why Ryan Trahan's Editing Is So Boring
- **Source:** 4.8 | Editor Josh | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y9RA0lpkprg | 962 s (16:02) | ~3,000 words | direct match (1.0). Long-form personality vlog; ad reads at [01:00], [05:30]–[06:00], [15:00]–[15:31].
- **Thesis:** Trahan's edit breaks "cut all dead air" rules on purpose. Restraint, authenticity, music contrast, leitmotifs and well-timed holds make it feel genuine; the edit stays dynamic through micro-changes (punch-ins, captions, moving graphics) rather than constant cutting.

**Rules**
1. Long awkward pauses can be left in deliberately when they are the joke or the character; it does not feel boring when it is intentional. [00:00]
2. Stand out by doing the opposite of the feed: Trahan's intros open on **dead silence** while everyone else opens loud. [00:30–01:00]
3. Deliberately unpolished visuals (a plain photo instead of slick VFX) signal genuineness when authenticity is the brand. [01:00–01:31]
4. State what is at stake early. [01:31]
5. **Contrast music**: alternate dramatic music with chill, loopable music; without the contrast the video feels flat. [01:31–02:00]
6. Make a single tripod shot feel multi-camera through many cuts with reframes/punch-ins, zooms, and text placed on objects. [02:00–02:32]
7. An unnecessary time-lapse can add relatable, home-made texture. [02:32]
8. **Leitmotif**: reuse the same music cue every time a place, character or idea returns, so the sound triggers recognition. [03:00]
9. Video-game music works as a bed because it is designed to loop for hours without fatigue. [03:00–03:31]
10. Hold shots long ("cutting without cutting"): every caption change is a micro-cut in the lower half of the frame. [03:31–04:00]
11. Do not caption every word, and avoid bouncy one-word-at-a-time captions; past a point they break immersion and authenticity. [04:00]
12. Self-aware editing (a goofy edit against a stern suit) feels authentic because it breaks the expected script. [04:31–05:00]
13. Cut interviews down to the one line that answers or lands; do not let them run once the gist is clear. [05:00–05:30]
14. Very fast cutting (eyes darting to read everything) is acceptable only when it is mixed among plenty of slower cuts; fast for the whole video is bad. [06:00–06:32]
15. Keep the camera on the host **static** (no slow push-ins/pull-outs) for a genuine look, while **graphics are always slowly moving** (in, out, down). [06:32–07:01]
16. The editor, not the host, decides how long an awkward beat runs; copying the "music cuts out, talk too long" bit without timing falls flat. Tell the host when a take is mid. [07:32–08:02]
17. Undercut over-sensationalised framing; honesty plays well with audiences used to being deceived. [08:02]
18. Fourth-wall edit gags (the host addresses the editor and the edit answers) give the editor a moment to show off. [08:30–09:01]
19. Dramatic music plus a zoom-in sells a comic beat. [09:31]
20. Hold a shot uncomfortably long when the discomfort is the point; finding that timing is the editor's job. [10:03]
21. **Rule of three**: three people say "no", then break the pattern. [10:03–10:32]
22. Repeat the rule-of-three structure later for a callback. [11:02]
23. Occasionally break the edit on purpose (an upbeat time-lapse cut off after ~2 s) for fun. [11:31–12:01]
24. Break immersion now and then; clients may love it. [12:01]
25. Trahan's transitional "review" music cue sits about **4 dB louder** than the other music; this imperfection is part of the flair. [12:31]
26. Build tension music, then cut to the least expected shot for the payoff (the restaurant is closed). [13:00]
27. Keep imperfect deliveries when they are funnier than perfect ones. [13:30]
28. Use a plain default font in **all lowercase**, like a text message, to signal authenticity. [14:00]
29. Use a leitmotif to foreshadow the payoff: replaying an earlier theme signals what will happen next. [14:31–15:31]

Rule count: 29.

### B.2 Short-form ad translation (9:16, 30–60 s talking-head Meta ad)

**Transfers directly**
- **Rules 1–4 are the ad's first 3 seconds.** Meta's "3-second video play" metric (hook rate = 3 s plays / impressions; an industry norm, not from these videos) makes 0–3 s the most valuable real estate. Spec for a talking-head ad hook:
  - 0.0–0.5 s: a visual event on frame 1. Not a static still: a punch-in, a slight push, a whip into a graphic, or a bright, saturated graphic card against the dark series look. (4.2 [17:30]: a slight zoom-in suffices.)
  - 0.0–2.0 s: the topic is spoken and also shown as on-screen text (4.1 [03:02]: on-screen text carries hooks when the voice is slow), e.g. the name of the service or the business category.
  - Sentence 1, "you"-framed pain: e.g. "If customers can't find your shop on Google…" (4.1 [09:00–10:30]).
  - Sentences 2–3, contrast: what they do now (A: word of mouth, a stale listing) vs what we do (B: faster, cheaper, more calls), stated or implied (4.1 [12:01–14:01]).
  - The hook is 2–3 lines and done by about 5–6 s (4.1 [14:01]).
- **Rule 5 (proof first):** put the most extraordinary true fact in the first 3 s: a real result, a real number, a real client name, or a screenshot. In code, the first cutaway should be the proof card, not a logo.
- **Rule 6 (close the loop):** open the loop in the hook and pay it off before the CTA (e.g. "how the shop next door gets the calls" → show the fix → then CTA). A CTA that arrives before the payoff spends the viewer's goodwill on an open question.
- **Rule 7:** mid-ad re-hooks ("but here's the part most owners miss…") every few seconds keep the "what will I miss" pressure on; in 30–60 s this means one re-hook roughly every beat.
- **Rule 9 (story-show):** this is the house cutaway system. Lists build on the spoken word, numbers count up (EDIT-STANDARD §1).
- **Rule 10 (rhythm variety):** in 30–60 s, variety means alternating A-roll (2–4 s) with cutaways (3–8 s) and having at least one tempo change (e.g. a fast problem montage, then a calmer solution/proof beat), not a single metronomic cut length.
- **Rule 11 (contrast):** a sparser sound bed under A-roll vs layered SFX on the cutaway whip-ins (adapted from 4.5's "naked" talking heads; see B.7 for the music conflict).
- **Rule 12:** any sentence that carries no new idea is cut. In a 45 s ad there is no room for setup without conflict or information.
- **Rules 13, 14, 16, 17, 19:** already house policy (motivated graphics, voice chain, frame design, series look, small 2-line captions).
- **Rule 18 (static host, moving graphics):** matches the house: static selfie A-roll with a 1.15× punch-in on alternate cuts, and graphics with slow drift.
- **Rule 20 (three):** lists of three (three problems, three services) are natural in ads; a pattern break on the third can carry the payoff.
- **Rule 21 (zenith):** an ad's zenith is the proof/result moment; give it the cleanest frame and the biggest sound hit, and do not clutter it.
- **Rule 23 (guardrail):** for local-business ads, fake urgency or fake stakes destroy trust. Use only real stakes ("every week your listing is wrong, calls go to competitors").
- **Rule 24 (likability):** the presenter should be on camera in the first frame or second, smiling and well lit; solving a real problem is the strongest lever.

**Does NOT transfer, or transfers only partly**
- 17 s holds and withholding the talking head for a minute (4.5 [00:00, 03:32]): long-form only. An ad has 30–60 s total; EDIT-STANDARD caps A-roll shots at 2–4 s and cutaways at 3–8 s.
- Long awkward pauses, dead-silence intros and comedic holds (4.8): personality long form only; they conflict with the house 0.25 s gap rule and the music bed. A silent first second in a sound-on Reels feed is a risk, not a differentiator, for an ad.
- Antagonists, time limits, competitions, bets (4.6 tactics 4, 8, 10, 16): challenge-video devices. In an ad only the honest versions transfer (the competitor shop as the "antagonist", real cost of inaction as the "stakes").
- Withholding the hero image for most of the video (4.5 [01:30–04:31]): in an ad the proof must come early (rule 5); withhold at most for one short beat inside the hook (e.g. blur or cover the result for under 2 s, then reveal).
- Levels 5–6 of the dopamine ladder (affection, revelation): build over many ads, not within one. They matter for a recurring presenter across a campaign.
- Fourth-wall editor gags, laugh tracks, deliberately "broken" edits (4.8): off-brand for a premium, designed local-business ad.
- Plain lowercase default font (4.8 [14:00]): conflicts with the house typography standard.
- Highlight reel of months (4.6 [15:31]): only as a before/after montage compressed to a few seconds.

### B.5 QC (automatable gates)

Signals available: whisper.cpp word timings (`w.start`, `w.end`, text), the render EDL (cut list, cutaway in/out, punch-in events, text-layer in/out from `render_v2.py`), ffmpeg frame difference / scene score, YuNet face boxes, librosa onsets, ebur128 on stems.

Definition used below, **visual event** = any of: A-roll cut, punch-in/reframe, cutaway start or end, a new graphic element appearing (list line, number, card), a transition. Caption text changes are logged separately as **micro-events** (4.8 [03:31]) and do NOT count toward visual-event gates.

### B.6 Anti-patterns / failure modes

| Anti-pattern | Symptom (how it shows up) | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Delay** | R1 > 2 s; greeting, name or "so today…" before the topic | Delete lead-in lines; start on the topic sentence. [4.1] |
| **Vague suspense hook** | R14 hit; viewer cannot say what the ad is about at 2 s | Replace with a topic + pain sentence; keep suspense for sentence 2. [4.1] |
| **Confusing hook** | FK grade > 6, passive voice, more than one reading | Reorder to reading order, simpler words, active voice. [4.1] |
| **"I" hook** | R11 fails ("I've helped 200 businesses…") | Reframe to "If your shop…". [4.1] |
| **Nice-to-know topic** | Trends/facts rather than a pain the owner has | Lead with the pain (missed calls, bad reviews, invisible listing). [4.1] |
| **No contrast / no loop** | Hook states a topic but raises no question | Add A vs B or a surprising number. [4.1, 4.2] |
| **Static first frame** | R3 > 1 s or R4 at noise floor; the ad opens on a held, unmoving face | Start on a push/punch-in or an animated card from frame 1. [4.2] |
| **Same-as-feed opening** | R5 fails; the first frame looks like every other selfie video | Use the series' signature graphic or colour accent in second 1. [4.2] |
| **Promise without proof** | No proof tag before 5 s; "we'll show you" language | Move the strongest result/number to the first 3 s. [4.6] |
| **Open loop never closed** | R16 fails; viewer left with an unanswered question | Add the answer line before the CTA or remove the question. [4.2] |
| **CTA before payoff** | R27 fails | Reorder: payoff → CTA. [4.2] |
| **Dead stretch** | R7 > 4 s or R8 > 8 s; a talking head with no new visual or idea | Add a cutaway that story-shows the line, or cut the line. [4.5, 4.6] |

| **Off-sync SFX** | R20 fails | Snap to the event frame; time-stretch the SFX to the visual. [4.3] |

### B.7 Conflicts & open questions (vs each other and vs EDIT-STANDARD.md)

1. **Front-load proof (4.6) vs withhold the reveal (4.5, 4.2 anticipation).** 4.6 says put the extraordinary thing first; 4.5 withholds the real card for most of the video; 4.2 says peak dopamine comes just before the answer. **Call for ads:** front-load *proof that the ad is worth watching* (a result or number) in 0–3 s, and run anticipation only on the *mechanism* ("here's how"), closing it before the CTA. A withhold may last at most one short beat. Withholding the main result for most of a 30–60 s ad loses viewers at the 3 s mark.
2. **Kill all dead air (4.3 social default; EDIT-STANDARD §3: no gap > 0.25 s) vs long awkward pauses and dead-silence intros (4.8) and 17 s holds (4.5).** **Call:** the house rule stands for ads. 4.8 and 4.5 are long-form personality/documentary choices where the story has earned the hold. No exception for ads unless a script deliberately writes a comic beat, which is off-brand.
3. **"Naked" talking heads with no music (4.5 [00:30]) and silent intros (4.8 [00:30]) vs a music bed under the voice throughout (EDIT-STANDARD §3; level from style `sound.bed_lu`, −7 LU for the first test client).** **Call:** keep the bed (the first reviewer asked three times for louder music), but borrow the *contrast* principle through dynamics: sparser SFX on A-roll and layered SFX hits on cutaway whip-ins (gate R22). **Open question for the user:** would they accept a 0.3–0.5 s bed drop (not silence) just before the proof reveal, as a "build and break" (4.5 [07:31])? It was not tested on the first test ad.
4. **Stimulation needs bright, saturated, contrasting first frames (4.2) vs a dark look (e.g. preset `profitcast-dark`: "warm, dark and moody", black background).** **Call:** not a real conflict if the *first second* carries the look's brightest element (e.g. a glow, accent/white type, a punch-in) over the A-roll. Gate R5 checks the opening against the body, not against an absolute brightness, so the dark look survives. Watch for an opening on a plain dark A-roll frame.
5. **Authentic and unpolished (4.8: default lowercase font, imperfect takes, cheap visuals) vs the house premium designed look (EDIT-STANDARD §1–2: real wordmarks, Material icons, series graphics).** **Call:** house wins. 4.8's authenticity applies to personality vlogs. What transfers is restraint: a static camera on the presenter, no gimmicky captions, no over-sensationalising.
6. **Caption micro-cuts (4.8 [03:31]) vs captions as minimal (EDIT-STANDARD §2: small, white, 2 lines).** Compatible: caption changes give a little movement, but gates do not count them as visual events (R7), so they cannot hide a dead stretch.
7. **Story-showing with long B-roll runs (4.5 withholds A-roll for a full minute) vs EDIT-STANDARD §1 40–55% cutaways, 3–8 s each.** **Call:** house ratios stand; story-showing is achieved within the 3–8 s cutaway units.
8. **Rapid-fire pacing as default (4.3) vs rhythm variety (4.5, 4.8).** Not a contradiction: 4.3 itself recommends a pattern break for serious/emotional moments [01:31]. **Call:** default tight (house 0.25 s gaps), with at least one tempo change per ad (gate R10). R10's thresholds are a proposal and should be calibrated on the user's approved inspiration edits before being made blocking.
9. **Retention editing as the goal (4.4, 4.6) vs retention is not enjoyment (4.7).** For ads the real metric is action (calls, clicks), not just watch-time. **Call:** use the retention tactics, but gate against fake urgency (B.6) and make the honest payoff and CTA the target.
10. **Music track switching to match every mood (4.6 [10:31], 4.5) vs a single house bed.** **Open question:** in 30–60 s, one music track with an energy change at the problem → solution turn (a stem or arrangement change) is likely enough; a hard track switch risks feeling like two ads. Needs a test.
11. **4.8's transition cue ~4 dB louder than the bed** is a personality-flair imperfection; it conflicts with house loudness discipline (−14 LUFS, a controlled bed). Not adopted.
12. **"Promise, proof, path" (4.4)** is named but never explained; it is consistent with 4.1 + 4.6 but cannot be cited as an independent source.
13. **Numeric thresholds.** The videos give only: topic and stimulation in the first 1–2 s (4.1, 4.2), ~200 ms bottom-up processing (4.2), sixth-grade reading level (4.1), 2–3-line hooks (4.1), ~20% reaching the end of "watch till the end" videos (4.6), >70% vs 30–40% retention benchmarks (4.7), a 17 s hold (4.5) and ~4 dB (4.8). Every other number in B.5 is either from EDIT-STANDARD or an explicitly labelled house proposal/industry norm, and should be calibrated against the user's approved edits.
14. **EDIT-STANDARD has no retention section.** Its gates cover words, pauses, stutters, loudness, picture integrity and layout, but none of hook timing, loop closure, visual-event spacing, proof placement or rhythm variety. **Recommendation:** add R1, R3, R6, R7, R15, R16 and R27 to §4 as blocking gates; add the rest as report-only until calibrated.
