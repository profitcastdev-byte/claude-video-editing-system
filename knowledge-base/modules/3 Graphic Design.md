### 3.1 Master 5 Design Principles With This Course! (MUST WATCH)
- **Source:** 3.1 · "Master 5 Design Principles With This Course! (MUST WATCH)" · Satori Graphics · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FpwZTl3dCZE · 1776 s (29:36) · ~4,800 words · match: direct (match 1), on topic. Compilation of repetition, contrast, hierarchy, white space and balance, then a 42-point quiz; one Skillshare ad read [27:34–28:31] (ignored).
- **Thesis:** Five principles (repetition, contrast, hierarchy, space, balance) turn a design from disjointed guesswork into a unified piece that attracts, intrigues and then delivers a message; using principles replaces trial and error.

**Repetition**
1. Repeat elements to glue a design into one unified piece; repeat colours, typeface styles/fonts, line, size and scale, shape, texture, and even language, not just "three squares in a row". [00:35]
2. Apply repetition to posters, portfolios, websites, editorial and whole brand identities; fix it in the brand-strategy planning stage. [01:01]
3. Use a constant colour pair over years so the scheme itself becomes a mental trigger for the brand (Coca-Cola red and white, 100+ years). [01:33]
4. Repeat an imagery style (Rogue: hand-drawn illustration + typographic logo; social feed of fruit, ingredients, sunsets, natural settings) until the consumer recognises the brand without conscious thought. [02:02]
5. Across a multi-page piece, reuse the same fonts, colour scheme, shapes and layout; this makes the viewer comfortable and signals competence. [02:34]
6. Anti-pattern: designs with no repetition read as "disjointed, unorganized", as if random things were applied. [03:05]
7. Keep a neutral base (black and white) everywhere and reserve colour for the few things of importance; colour then highlights them (portfolio showreel example). [03:05]
8. Use the same typeface styles throughout so the work reads as made by one designer. [03:35]
9. Carry a background texture and an interaction style (connected-typography rollover) across every page, not one. [04:03]
10. Break repetition deliberately to create a focal point, but only with repetition surrounding it (all-angular site; the one hover target is circular). [04:03]
11. Break an industry's repetition to stand out in a crowded category (UK banks are black/red/blue; Lloyds uses black and green, since the 1980s). [05:06]
12. Repetition is not mandatory on every design; sometimes go against it, but it is the default. [05:34]

**Contrast**
13. Use contrast of colour (white on black, white on peach) as the first and most obvious contrast. [06:03]
14. Use contrast of shape: straight, angled, pointy lines around a smooth curved product/logo make the product and logo stand out. [06:34]
15. Use contrast of texture: a sandy, blurred background texture against a smooth, realistic product draws the eye to the product. [07:32]
16. Mirror light/dark between two faces of a piece (dark background + white type on one side, inverse on the other). [08:03]
17. Use different text weights and different text sizes; this is contrast and also hierarchy. [08:32]
18. Use high-contrast labels (black labels on white/grey) that stand out everywhere in a UI. [09:33]
19. Mark the most important item with a contrasting font choice, weight and colour together (why "for sale" signs are red). [10:00]
20. Use "double" contrast: a bold bright colour block on a white/grey background, with images inside whose colours contrast each other (red on blue, blue on orange, turquoise on pink). [10:00]
21. Small UI accents (red notification dots, tabs) are contrast too. [10:37]

**Hierarchy**
22. Build hierarchy from dominance and priority: give extra weight to some elements over others so the message lands and readers can navigate. [11:03]
23. Structure the viewer's path in three stages: attract, intrigue, deliver the message. [11:34]
24. Example order: product + pink splash attracts; the quote "time to change up" intrigues; the copy below delivers. The design is "a visual trap". [11:34]
25. Make level 1 the largest and brightest elements (iPhone image + word "iPhone"); level 2 the tagline; level 3 the paragraph. [12:36]
26. Let the hero image point the eye to the text (the phone directs the eye to the word). [12:36]
27. Not every section needs level 1; a section can carry only levels 2 and 3. [13:01]
28. Split a design into sections, each with two or three hierarchy levels: L1 headings + main imagery, L2 subtitles + other graphics, L3 body text + small details. [13:32]
29. Decide the levels and the number of sections (possibly via grids) before choosing the hierarchy technique. [13:32]
30. Use several hierarchy techniques at once. The list: weight (bolder for important), size (more important = bigger), colour (different colour stands out), orientation (e.g. isometric lower-left to upper-right with an opposing angle), white space (room to breathe makes it more important), font style (serif body, sans-serif heading). [14:05]

**Space**
31. Use space to enhance hierarchy, prevent clutter, add elegance and strengthen proximity bonds. [15:33]
32. Start from the content you were given; distinguish macro white space (large empty areas) from micro white space (between lines, paragraphs and tightly packed objects). [16:07]
33. With lots of text, focus on micro white space; macro still matters, just less. [16:31]
34. Balance micro vs macro for the audience; for viewers glancing while walking briskly (subway poster), tip toward more macro white space so it digests at first glance. [17:00]
35. More macro white space increases emphasis on the focal point and noticeability. [17:31]
36. Rule of thumb: more macro reads minimal, modern, luxury; more micro reads informative and serious. [17:31]
37. Use passive white space (micro spacing for legibility and aesthetics) and active white space (spacing that sets reading order and structure). [17:31]
38. Fix an illegible paragraph first with passive space, then break it into digestible chunks with active space so the viewer moves through it and stays longer. [18:30]

**Balance**
39. Balance visual weight across the vertical and horizontal axes; it gives stability and even distribution. [19:01]
40. Use symmetrical balance (mirrored shapes/type, comfortable) or asymmetrical balance (hierarchy creates the balance). [19:01]
41. Balance can work in groups of three, not only pairs: three large central circles balanced by type, fine-line graphics and small elements. [19:33]
42. Balance one large singular element (a person) with a cluster of small elements in the opposite (top-left) area. [20:03]

**Quiz answers (each is a rule)**
43. The main CTA uses emphasis. [20:31]
44. A big front/back difference on a card is contrast. [21:11]
45. Illustration far right + logo top left is balance. [21:36]
46. An illusion in the focal point is negative space (a subcategory of space). [22:02]
47. Items understood as belonging together because they are grouped is proximity; the viewer links them subconsciously as one. [22:35]
48. To fix weak typography: create font contrast (change one of the fonts) and create size hierarchy. [23:35]
49. Hierarchy by colour: first name bright white, surname muted; by size: name larger than the text above; the main graphic larger than everything else. Five uses on one page. [24:37]
50. A square graphic repeated on every magazine page is repetition (a repeated colour scheme also counts). [25:02]
51. Lines that guide the viewer's eye along a design (lines from a camera) are rhythm. [25:33]
52. Pastel colours are psychologically positive, linked to cleanliness and freshness; fit health, makeup, fragrance; carry feminine connotations. [26:31]
53. Score bands: 42 points total, above 30 great, above 21 a pass. [27:05]
54. Design from principles, not trial and error: trial and error wastes time and does not guarantee a good result. [28:31]

### 3.2 How To MASTER 1 Design Principle In 4 Minutes!
- **Source:** 3.2 · "How To MASTER 1 Design Principle In 4 Minutes!" · Satori Graphics · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_LCEw4jCq1o · 234 s (3:54) · ~700 words · match: direct, on topic (seven hierarchy hacks).
- **Thesis:** Hierarchy is the principle to master; seven hacks (leading lines, F pattern, Z pattern, typographic hierarchy, three layers, colour, rule of odds) control what is seen first, second and third.

1. Bigger objects are noticed first and so read as more important. [00:00]
2. Use leading lines from the dominant visual to point at the next important asset (buildings pointing up to the text). [00:00]
3. Use depth-of-field lines to draw the viewer into the secondary asset. [00:35]
4. A centred layout is "not a terrible design" but can be improved with a reading pattern. [00:35]
5. Use the F pattern: command hierarchy from top-left across to the right (left-to-right readers). [01:00]
6. The F pattern leaves white space at the far right; put the main CTA there. [01:00]
7. Not every hack fits every design; use them situationally. [01:00]
8. Use the Z pattern: top-left, across right, diagonal down, across right again; place headline, images and CTA along that path. [01:00]
9. Typographic hierarchy can "make or break" a design; always bring hierarchy to text. [01:32]
10. Fix flat type with hierarchy of colour and size, keeping alignment to the left. [02:00]
11. If in doubt, think in three layers of importance: 1 focal point, 2 secondary, 3 tertiary. [02:00]
12. Apply the three layers recursively: a single panel can have its own three layers (Pinterest ads). [02:30]
13. Use colour for hierarchy: a uniquely coloured element (the moon) is the focal point. [02:30]
14. Repeat the focal accent colour downstream (orange word, then more orange below) to move the eye down the design. [03:00]
15. Rule of odds: an odd number of objects (5) with one dominant focal object composes better; 4 objects is harder to make appealing in any layout. [03:00]
16. The rule of odds works best with a single dominant object. [03:34]

### 3.3 Learn EVERY Graphic Design Grid In 8 Minutes!
- **Source:** 3.3 · "Learn EVERY Graphic Design Grid In 8 Minutes!" · Satori Graphics · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gUzkRue28VI · 500 s (8:20) · ~1,400 words · match: direct, on topic (13 grids with a bonus tip each).
- **Thesis:** Thirteen grid systems give structure; each works best when bent slightly (span, vary, overlap, offset) without losing order.

1. Baseline grid: align all body text, headlines and captions across columns to one baseline grid for consistent spacing and readability. [00:00]
2. Make headline sizes a multiple of the base leading: 28 pt headlines for 14 pt body text keeps baseline alignment and a harmonious relationship. [00:00]
3. Column grid: divide the page into four equal vertical sections (as described) for magazines, newspapers, multi-content sites. [00:34]
4. Break the column grid slightly: let a featured image span three columns while text flows in one. [01:05]
5. Modular grid: give each product/graphic its own module (image, title, description, price) for consistency across pages. [01:05]
6. Vary module size by importance: give best-sellers/new arrivals more space, e.g. span two modules horizontally, to break monotony while staying organised. [01:34]
7. Manuscript grid: a single column for book text, with margins for notes or images, for distraction-free reading. [02:00]
8. Hierarchical grid: set up differently sized modules first, put large images and headlines/CTAs in big blocks, navigation/footers less prominently, then add content. [02:30]
9. In a hierarchical grid, contrast primary and secondary by colour and size: headline bright and bold, subheads/nav muted and smaller. [02:30]
10. Golden ratio about 1:1.618 for proportion (Twitter bird, Apple logo). [03:04]
11. Do not become attached to a grid; use the golden ratio as a starting point, then adjust by eye for the project. [03:30]
12. Asymmetric grid: let the hero image or headline take more space to draw attention; smaller columns carry secondary information. [04:00]
13. Layer: let text overlap images slightly, or add subtle diagonal lines, for depth and motion. [04:00]
14. Square grid: uniform squares (Instagram profile, galleries) are satisfying and easy to navigate. [04:35]
15. Add a pop of colour or subtle pattern behind certain squares for interest without breaking the grid. [05:02]
16. Rule of thirds: two horizontal + two vertical lines make nine equal parts; place key elements on them. [05:02]
17. Break the thirds deliberately: put the main subject slightly off the grid for tension or surprise. [05:33]
18. Compound grid: combine a column grid (listings) with a modular grid (details). [06:00]
19. Never cram when combining grids; use white space strategically to separate elements (applies to every grid). [06:00]
20. Isometric grid: 3D look for buildings, data structures, infographics. [06:00]
21. On isometric shapes use lighter colours on top and darker underneath for volume. [06:34]
22. Circular grid: for round/organic logos; symmetry and balance. [06:34]
23. A circular grid also creates interesting shapes in the spaces between elements. [07:04]
24. Triangular grid: geometric patterns, or place logo and product details at specific points (product box). [07:04]
25. Pair a triangular grid with bright colours/textures, e.g. a gradient flowing along the triangle angles or foil accents where elements meet. [07:34]

### 3.4 Typography Tutorial - 10 rules to help you rule type
- **Source:** 3.4 · "Typography Tutorial - 10 rules to help you rule type" · The Futur (design: Chris Do) · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QrNi9FmdlxY · 214 s (3:34) · ~620 words (on-screen text transcribed) · match: direct, on topic.
- **Thesis:** Ten hard typographic rules, all serving contrast and spacing: left alignment, one font, skip weights, double sizes, one axis, classic faces, rules for grouping, avoid corners, mind the gaps, be bold or italic.

1. When in doubt, set type flush left, rag right; readers go top to bottom, left to right, and the eye finds the left edge. [00:00]
2. Do not indent the first line of a paragraph (it breaks the left edge). [00:00]
3. Use one font; two fonts need real understanding that they complement. [00:00]
4. Never pair two fonts of the same classification (two sans, two serifs, two slabs, two scripts); the reason is contrast. [00:30]
5. Stay with one font until you have mastered it (Helvetica Neue example). [00:30]
6. Skip a weight when changing weights: light to bold, or medium to extra bold. [00:30]
7. Slight weight changes are hard for the audience to notice; contrast is the key to great design. [00:30]
8. Try bold headline + light body copy. [00:30]
9. When changing point size, double or halve it: 30 pt headline with 15 pt body. [01:01]
10. For more drama use 3x or 4x the size. [01:01]
11. Build type along one primary axis and align elements to that line. [01:01]
12. For a vertical axis, align the left edges of type; works at any font or size. [01:01]
13. For a horizontal axis, align on the strongest horizontal element: sometimes cap height, sometimes baseline. [01:01]
14. Pick from proven faces: Akzidenz Grotesk, Avenir, Avant Garde, Bell Gothic, Bodoni, Bembo, Caslon, Clarendon, Courier, DIN Mittelschrift, Franklin Gothic, Frutiger, Futura, Garamond, Gill Sans, Gotham, Helvetica, Letter Gothic, Memphis, Meta, OCR-B, Rockwell, Sabon, Trade Gothic, Trajan, Univers. [01:32]
15. Use rules (lines) to group related blocks; they also make dissimilar objects look orderly. [01:32]
16. Avoid the corners: do not place elements on the page edge or corners unless deliberately cropping them. [01:32]
17. Negative space is good; let the design breathe. [02:06]
18. Never use forced-justified type: it creates rivers. [02:06]
19. No widows: never a single word alone on the last line of a paragraph. [02:06]
20. No orphans: never let a new column/page start with the last word or line of the previous paragraph. [02:34]
21. One space after punctuation, never two. [02:34]
22. Watch the shape the rag makes; avoid undesired shapes and angles. [02:34]
23. Spacing matters: the closer things are, the more the reader assumes they are related; text with no word spaces is unreadable ("Spacingmatters"). [03:00]
24. Be bold or italic, never regular. [03:00]

### 3.5 How to Arrange Text in a Design (Ep 4) | Foundations of Graphic Design
- **Source:** 3.5 · "How to Arrange Text in a Design (Ep 4) | Foundations of Graphic Design | Adobe Creative Cloud" · Adobe Creative Cloud · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fEILVYJkB4M · 263 s (4:23) · ~640 words · match: direct, on topic. Directly about scroll-feed social graphics.
- **Thesis:** Beyond hierarchy, alignment and proximity (where text sits relative to other text and to the frame edges) decide whether a layout feels calm and organised or murky and disconnected.

1. Arrange text deliberately: one project has many text elements doing different jobs; design how the chunks work and feel together. [00:00]
2. In a scrolling feed a key element catches attention, the eye moves to a secondary spot, then keeps scanning; meanwhile the viewer forms a gut emotional feeling. Layout guides both. [00:30]
3. Clear hierarchy is "super important", but alignment and proximity matter too. [00:30]
4. Alignment (lining elements up consistently) makes things feel organised and calm. [01:01]
5. Proximity: closer elements seem related; that helps viewers process the frame. [01:01]
6. Practise by moving text only (no size or property changes) and judging alignment and proximity. [01:31]
7. Decide how close headings are to body text, how close display text is to the rest, how close blurbs are to each other. [02:00]
8. There is usually one strong axis of alignment for the whole layout; check the whole group's alignment. [02:31]
9. Also check alignment pair by pair at the lower level. [02:31]
10. Do not trust tool snapping; things must look visually aligned. [02:31]
11. Headings too close to body text make things murky; headings closer to each other than to their own body text associate wrongly. [03:02]
12. A layout that is too spacious feels disconnected and messy. [03:02]
13. Frame boundaries change everything: if elements sit closer to the edges than to each other, it is a problem; tighten the group. [03:30]
14. Repeat the exercise and compare solutions to train judgement. [04:00]

### 3.6 Graphic Design Principles Master-Class! (Ep 1 White Space)
- **Source:** 3.6 · "Graphic Design Principles Master-Class! (Ep 1 White Space)" · Satori Graphics · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FDFD2-poyGw · 614 s (10:14) · ~1,600 words · match: direct, on topic. Noun Project ad read [05:01–06:30] ignored.
- **Thesis:** White space is an active system in six stages: breathing room, grouping, focus, a micro/macro spacing scale, emotion, and advanced tension and tempo.

1. White space is the space between elements; it is active, not empty. [00:00]
2. Do not panic and fill blank space (a beginner and beginner-client habit). [00:32]
3. Add generous padding around text blocks, space between sections and room around the logo; the design calms down without adding any element. [00:32]
4. White space creates balance, structure and confidence. [01:01]
5. Use white space to create groups/sections (Gestalt proximity): close = group, spaced = separate. [01:32]
6. Group a heading with its subheading, then leave more space before the body text. [01:32]
7. Squint test: blur your view of the layout; you should still see which elements belong together before reading a word. [02:01]
8. White space need not be empty: background textures, soft images or subtle colour blocks still act as white space if they keep breathing room. Perception of separation is what counts. [02:30]
9. Spacing alone directs focus, with no arrows, colours or graphics. [03:01]
10. To highlight a button, give it generous breathing room. [03:01]
11. To tie a heading to its paragraph, reduce that gap. [03:01]
12. Poor hierarchy makes the viewer work; smart spacing guides them. [03:01]
13. Micro white space: around text and small sections (letter spacing, line height, component padding). Macro: margins, gutters, space between large sections. [03:31]
14. Use one spatial scale, a consistent set of values (spoken as "4 8632", i.e. 4, 8, [16], 32), across micro and macro layers; it builds rhythm, trust and cohesion. [04:02]
15. Worked example: 4 pt between body lines, 8 pt heading-to-body, 32 pt from the whole text group to the image/asset. [04:30]
16. Micro space is for comfortable reading and fine navigation; macro space sets visual style and movement around the work. [04:30]
17. White space carries emotion: lots of space reads premium, modern, minimal (Apple); tight, chaotic spacing reads energetic, raw, non-conformist (punk flyer). [06:30]
18. Decide the feeling first (clean and calm, energetic and raw, playful and quirky), then set spacing to build it. [07:02]
19. Imbalance done on purpose creates focus with attitude (symmetry vs asymmetry). [07:30]
20. Dynamic counterbalance: push one element far into space (small logo in the lower left of a huge poster); the eye actively searches for weight. [08:00]
21. Anchor every off-balance layout with a visual counterpoint (small text block, shadow, line); otherwise tension becomes confusion. [08:30]
22. Cover test: cover parts of the layout to see if the tension still holds and the space pulls/pushes the eye on purpose. [08:30]
23. White space creates tempo; vary the intervals to control how fast the eye moves (spatial pacing). [09:00]
24. Rule of thumb: tight spacing = fast, urgent beats; wide spacing = slow, dramatic pauses; unexpected gaps interrupt expectation. [09:30]
25. In a vertical scroll, alternate breathing space and sudden tightening; that contrast creates rhythm. [09:30]

### 3.7 The ONLY Colour Theory Video You Ever Need To Watch! (replacement for "Principles of Graphic Design: Colors")
- **Source:** 3.7 · "🔸 The ONLY Colour Theory Video You Ever Need To Watch!" · Satori Graphics · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HKtTNOgfhAU · 1905 s (31:45) · ~5,500 words · match: manual replacement (header says it replaces the Daniel R. C. "Colors" course entry), fully on topic (colour). Sections: before/after, saturation and psychology, a method, Illustrator tricks, logo colour meanings, five tools, colour harmonies.
- **Thesis:** Pick colour from the emotion and message you want the target audience to feel, controlling saturation, brightness and contrast as much as hue, then build a harmony from one researched base colour.

**Choosing from emotion**
1. Choose colour from the emotion and message of the design, using colour psychology. [00:00]
2. Orange/yellow = energetic, excitement, change; red = danger, passion, warning; green = natural, tranquil, healthy. [01:01]
3. For lifestyle change and energetic movement (gym poster), orange is a strong choice; it also matches energetic angled lines. [01:01]
4. For skin care aimed at women, use pastel skin tones and pinks; pastels are open, relaxing, peaceful, soft, and relate to the product. [01:33]
5. Anti-pattern: raising saturation a lot on a beauty ad is "too aggressive". [02:35]
6. Relate colour to product, message and overall feeling. [02:35]
7. Use complementary colours (opposite on the wheel) for contrast; impactful and appealing together. [03:31]
8. Research: red more stimulating than green, green more than blue (K.W. Jacobs, 1974); colour affects EEG, heart rate and emotional perception (1981 study). [04:00]
9. Hue is not the only lever: brightness + low saturation = soft; dimness + high saturation = hard/strong. [04:32]
10. Weak contrast + weak saturation = calm; strong contrast + saturation = activity. [04:32]
11. Pastels feel calming because they lack saturation (hence baby products). [04:32]
12. Calm, trustworthy brands (insurance): low saturation and weak contrast. [05:05]
13. Know the feeling first, then set saturation, contrast and brightness. [05:34]
14. Red stimulates breathing, pulse, blood pressure and muscle tension, which aids recall and memory formation; brands use red to grab attention and build hard memories (CNN, Target). [06:01]
15. HubSpot test: a red CTA button beat a green one by 21% in click-through rate. [06:30]
16. Vibrant red + vibrant green together is unpleasant; lighten red to soft pink and make green less saturated and slightly darker for a memorable palette. [07:01]
17. Culture changes meaning: red = love in the West, wrath/anger in Japan. [07:30]
18. Age and gender: on average women prefer softer pastels, men bolder colours; design for the majority of the target audience. [07:30]
19. You must know the audience: age, gender, location, likes/dislikes, intended feeling. [08:00]

**Method**
20. Step 1: write down words for how you want the viewer to feel. [08:32]
21. Blue = security (finance brands); light soft blue = calming. Example feeling words: safe, secure, calm. [08:32]
22. Add hints of orange (action, movement) to push the viewer to take action and click. [09:00]
23. Every colour should make the viewer feel a defined way. [09:00]
24. Colour psychology is used by virtually every major brand; dismissing it is "ignorant". [09:34]
25. Energy + action copy ("accelerate") pairs with a strong bright yellow as the main colour; yellow = energy and positive change. [10:04]
26. Step 2: write words for the design's personality (e.g. lots of open white space + friendly fonts + handwritten note = clean, minimal, friendly). [10:33]
27. Step 3: map keywords to one, two or three colours via colour psychology. [11:01]
28. Step 4: start from one base colour from research (e.g. muted tame green for friendly/natural), then cycle harmonies (complementary, monochromatic...). [11:30]
29. Make the base colour the majority; use the harmony partner (pink, orange) for CTA and focal points. [12:01]
30. If the client gives a brand palette, use it. [12:34]
31. Colour is creative but methodical: viewer feeling, then personality, then scheme. [12:34]

**Tool tricks (Illustrator/Photoshop; translate to code)**
32. Shift-drag RGB sliders moves all channels together: quick tints/highlights. [13:04]
33. Use the complement function for an instant complementary colour (blue to orange). [13:33]
34. Recolour a whole design in one step (Edit Colors: grayscale, saturation). [14:00]
35. Use global colour swatches so one edit changes the colour everywhere. [14:00]

**Colour meanings for logos/brands**
36. Orange: invigorating, playful, stands out, positive change/action/energy. [15:33]
37. Red: excitement, passion, also anger; stands out; can read negative; use cautiously. [15:33]
38. Yellow: thoughtful, cheerful, energetic; very loud. [16:04]
39. Green: nature, growth, health; usable in almost any sector. [16:04]
40. Blue: trustworthy, mature, calming; use if the brand wants to be taken seriously. [16:35]
41. Purple: feminine, luxury, royalty, sophisticated. Pink: girly in the West but versatile; neon pink = energy, modern. [16:35]
42. Brown: rugged, masculine, serious; rare in logos. Black: slick, modern, luxurious, high-end. [17:03]
43. Return to the brand research and keywords before choosing; target the customer (Cadbury: 78% of Facebook engagers female, purple = feminine + luxury). [17:30]
44. Use one or two colours; beginners should stick to one or two. [19:04]
45. Many colours (Google, Microsoft) only when you know exactly what you are doing; one colour is great for recognition (Coca-Cola). Overuse is a common failure (Kraft rebrand). [19:04]
46. Use colour to stand out from a category (social apps are blue; Snapchat yellow). [20:01]
47. Know where the client and target market are: white = pure/clean in the West, death in parts of Asia. [20:30]
48. Use Adobe Color Explore with brief keywords to find targeted palettes (e.g. modern tech startup). [21:08]
49. Primary mixing, greyscale ramp, harmonies and tint/tone/shade of each hue are on a physical colour wheel; useful for hands-on learners and print. [22:04]
50. Recolor Artwork with the link on shifts a whole scheme at once for quick variations. [23:05]
51. Pigment: adjust brightness/saturation, get HEX/RGB/Pantone, make two-colour gradients and duotone images. [24:00]
52. Paletton: build harmonies and preview them on example website and graphics; export palettes. [25:02]
53. Photoshop: recolour a selection with a Hue/Saturation layer and the sampling tool, no masks. [26:03]
54. Hue = pure colour; tint = hue + white; tone = hue + white + black (as stated); shade = hue + black. [26:33]

**Harmonies**
55. Monochrome: one hue plus tints/tones/shades. Smooth, relaxing, no contrast, does not "pop"; do not use for urgent attention (sale signs, charts); good for digital art, some logos. [27:10]
56. Complementary: opposite hues (tints/tones/shades allowed). High contrast, attention-grabbing, can be jarring; use a hue + tint and their complements (4 colours) for depth and less jarring (Firefox, Fanta). [28:02]
57. Analogous: base + one either side (3), or two more either side (5). Low contrast; for soft designs; not for attention-grabbing signs. [29:00]
58. Triadic: three evenly spaced hues (move four spaces, three gaps). Very contrasting, can overpower; soften with tints/tones/shades; good for charts and infographics but do not let text get lost (Burger King). [30:03]
59. Split complementary: base + the two colours either side of its opposite; hard to balance, needs experimentation with tints; can work (example web page). [30:33]

### B.2 Short-form ad translation (9:16, 30–60 s, talking head, 1080x1920, PIL text)

- **Hierarchy (1, 17).** Each frame gets one L1 element. On A-roll, L1 is the face; text is L2/L3 (captions). On a cutaway, L1 is the big word/number/headline, L2 the supporting line, L3 small labels. Attract→intrigue→deliver maps to hook (0–3 s) → body → CTA card. Do not stack two L1s (big word + big number) in the same frame.
- **Size steps (2).** At 1080 px width, use a size ladder of 2x steps. Example derived from the 2x rule (not stated in the videos): caption ≈ 48 px, card body ≈ 48 px, card headline ≈ 96 px, hero word ≈ 144–192 px (3–4x). Never 52 vs 60 px for different levels.
- **Weight steps (3).** Load real weights (e.g. 300/700 or 500/800), not one weight faked by stroke. Same level = same weight across the whole ad.
- **Proximity (4).** Cutaway lists: line gap within an item < gap between items. A label stays next to its icon well. Captions must not drift close enough to the logo/CTA to read as part of it. Squint test translates to a blur gate (B.5).
- **Alignment (5).** Cutaway cards and lists: one left axis per card. Captions: one centre axis. Mixing a left-aligned card with a centred headline in the same frame breaks "one axis".
- **Frame boundary (6).** In Reels the frame edge is also the UI edge: the top has the account bar and the bottom has caption, CTA and icons; the right column has like/comment/share. "Avoid corners" is extra true. ES's 60 px margin is the minimum; Reels UI zones are larger (B.7).
- **Spacing scale (7).** Use one token set scaled to 1080 width, e.g. 8/16/32/64 px (the 4/8/[16]/32 scale doubled for a 1080 canvas; doubling is my derivation). Every gap in `gfx.py` should come from it.
- **Accent (8, 13).** The test look (preset `profitcast-dark`) is black + green glow + green-accent/white type. The style accent is the base; reserve one accent (by complementary logic a warm/pink-orange; 3.7 says orange = action) only for CTA/key number. Never colour random words.
- **Repetition (9).** The house series look *is* rule 9: same dot grid, card style, corner brackets, fonts and sizes in every cutaway and across ads, so viewers learn the format.
- **White space (14).** Reels viewers are the "brisk subway walker" of 3.1 [17:00]: favour macro space and one idea per cutaway; do not cram three cards into one frame.
- **Eye guidance (15).** In video, the order of appearance (build-on-word, ES §1) does the job of F/Z paths. Still useful: the speaker's gaze/gesture as a leading line toward a card; place the card on the side the speaker faces.
- **Tempo (18).** Spatial pacing maps onto edit pacing: dense, tight cutaways for urgent problem lists; a wide, empty frame with one big word as a "dramatic pause" before the CTA.
- **Colour psychology (12, 19, 23).** Match the base colour to the offer: e.g. for a growth/visibility service, green (growth, health) fits. Trust lines → calmer, less saturated treatment. CTA → saturated, contrasting accent. Never a monochrome-only CTA card. Regional audiences: 3.7 only names "parts of Asia" for white = death; avoid all-white mourning-looking full-frame cards (my judgement, verify locally).
- **Rule of odds / thirds (21, 22).** Lists of 3 or 5 items beat 4 when the script allows; face on a thirds line when text shares the frame.
- **Typesetting hygiene (16).** Caption chunks and card lines: no single-word last line, no double spaces, no forced justify.
- **Do not transfer:** golden ratio logo construction, isometric/circular/triangular grids, manuscript/baseline grids for long text, editorial orphans across columns/pages, Illustrator/Photoshop menu tricks, print colour wheels, Pantone. Little long-form text exists in a 30–60 s ad. Baseline-grid logic does transfer as "headline leading = integer multiple of body leading" on multi-line cards.

### B.3 Checklist

**Pre-edit (design tokens, once per series/ad)**
- [ ] Font set fixed: one family (or two of different classification), real weight files, weights at least two steps apart.
- [ ] Size ladder fixed with 2x steps (optional 3–4x hero size).
- [ ] Spacing token set fixed (one scale used for line gaps, padding, group gaps, margins).
- [ ] Palette fixed: base colour + at most one accent + neutrals; accent reserved for CTA/focal.
- [ ] Feeling words and personality words written for this client (3.7 method); colour choices traced to them.
- [ ] Cultural check of colours for the target city/community done.
- [ ] Safe zones defined (ES 60 px + Reels UI zones) and face box track available.

**During edit (per cutaway/text layer)**
- [ ] Exactly one L1 element in the frame.
- [ ] At most three hierarchy levels visible.
- [ ] All text in the card shares one alignment axis.
- [ ] Gaps inside groups are smaller than gaps between groups.
- [ ] Elements are not closer to the frame edge than to each other; nothing in corners.
- [ ] Accent colour appears only on the CTA/key figure.
- [ ] No widow (single-word last line), no double spaces, no justified text.
- [ ] List lengths odd where the script allows (3 or 5).
- [ ] Off-centre layouts have a counterweight element.
- [ ] Spacing tempo matches the beat (tight for urgency, wide for the pause before CTA).

**Pre-delivery**
- [ ] Every text gate in B.5 passed for every rendered text layer.
- [ ] Style tokens identical across all cutaways (repetition audit).
- [ ] Contrast gate passed on the actual rendered frame (not the design file).
- [ ] Squint/blur test passed on contact-sheet frames.
- [ ] No text inside Reels UI zones or ES 60 px margin; zero overlap with face box.

### B.7 Conflicts & open questions

1. **Flush-left (3.4) vs centred captions (ES "mid-chest" captions; vertical-video convention).** 3.4 says "when in doubt" justify left; 3.2 [00:35] says a centred layout is "not terrible". **Call:** centre single-axis captions (the centre line is the one axis, satisfying 3.4 rule 11); left-align multi-line cutaway cards and lists. Never mix both in one frame (Q10).
2. **ES 60 px safe area vs Reels UI.** The videos say avoid edges/corners (3.4) and that boundaries matter (3.5) but give no px. ES's 60 px only prevents clipping; it does not protect against Instagram's top bar, bottom caption/CTA panel and right action column. **Call:** keep ES's 60 px as the hard FAIL and add a Reels UI-zone WARN (FAIL for CTA/key numbers). The ~14% top / ~35% bottom figures are an industry norm, not from these videos; verify against current Meta guidance. Open question: ES puts captions "mid-chest"; on a typical selfie framing mid-chest can fall below y ≈ 1250, inside the bottom-35% zone. Measure the caption y on the real footage.
3. **"Be bold or italic, never regular" (3.4 rule 24) vs ES "captions: small, white, sentence case".** ES does not specify weight. **Call:** captions at Medium/Semibold (not Regular), which also helps contrast over moving video; display words Bold. Keep caption size small per ES. Open: confirm with the user that a heavier caption weight still matches the reference edits.
